FlipRadar is a set of free calculators and plain-English guides for the math every flip depends on: after repair value, your maximum offer, the rehab budget, financing, holding costs and what is actually left at the end. No account, no signup, nothing to buy.

The goal is simple: help you avoid overpaying. Most bad flips are not lost at the sale. They are lost at the offer, when the ARV was optimistic, the rehab was light, or the holding period was a guess.

## Key takeaways

- **Every flip comes down to one line:** sale price minus everything you spend, including the price you pay.
- **ARV is the number everything else depends on.** Get it from sold comps, adjusted, with a range, not a single number.
- **The 70% rule is a screen, not an offer.** Use it to discard deals quickly, then calculate your real maximum.
- **Profit is something you decide before the offer,** not what is left over after it.
- **Time is a cost.** Every extra month adds interest, taxes, insurance and utilities, and often an extension fee.
- **Published flip profits are usually gross.** The widely quoted ATTOM figure for Q2 2026 is a typical gross profit of $60,526, before rehab, financing, holding and selling costs.

## The formula behind every flip

```
Net profit = Sale price − Purchase price − Rehab − Buying costs
             − Holding costs − Financing costs − Selling costs

Maximum Purchase Price = ARV − Rehab − Buying, holding, financing and selling costs − Your profit
```

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar uses one sample house across its pages so you can follow a single deal from comps to sale. All numbers are hypothetical.

| Step | Result |
|---|---|
| House | 1,500 sq ft, 3 bed / 2 bath ranch, built 1968, asking $185,000 |
| ARV from adjusted comps | $295,000 |
| Rehab with 12% contingency | $70,000 |
| 70% rule maximum offer | $136,500 |
| Maximum Purchase Price at a $35,000 profit target | about $147,000 |
| Negotiated price | $145,000 |
| Net profit after financing, holding and selling (6 months) | about $37,300 |
| Same deal at 9 months with ARV 5% lower | about $14,300 |

The asking price was $40,000 above what the deal could support. The stress test at the bottom is the reason to run the numbers before the offer, not after.

## What the results mean

The calculators show estimates based only on the numbers you enter. A good result with optimistic inputs is not a good deal. When a deal only works with the best-case ARV, the lowest rehab bid and a fast sale, treat it as a pass, or as a lower offer.

## Assumptions

- You enter your own comps, bids, loan terms and local costs.
- Percentages in examples (closing costs, contingency, rule percentages) are illustrative. Replace them with local numbers.

## Common mistakes

1. **Starting from the asking price** instead of ARV.
2. **Using list prices as comps.**
3. **Treating the 70% rule as your offer.**
4. **Budgeting rehab with no contingency.**
5. **Planning a hold with no extra months.**
6. **Comparing your net profit to published gross figures.**
