A hard money loan is short-term, asset-based financing for flips and other rehab projects. You pay for speed and flexibility with a higher rate, upfront points and fees. The headline rate is rarely the full story. How the loan is sized, when rehab money is released, and what happens if you need more time often matter more.

This calculator estimates the total cost of a hard money loan from the loan amount, rate, points, term and fees. This page explains the parts lenders do not put in the headline: draws, leverage caps, borrower requirements, underwriting and extensions. Free, no account, nothing to buy.

## Key takeaways

- **Total cost = points + fees + interest.** Points and fees are paid even if you sell early. Interest depends on how long you hold and how much is drawn.
- **Your loan is the smallest of several caps:** a share of purchase price, a share of total cost (LTC) and a share of after repair value (LTARV). The lowest one wins.
- **Rehab money usually comes in draws, after the work is done and inspected.** You need cash to fund work before each draw.
- **Ask whether interest is charged on the full loan or only on funds drawn.** On the same loan, that one clause can change your cost by thousands.
- **Plan the exit inside the original term.** Extensions cost money and are not guaranteed.
- **Cash needed is more than the down payment:** closing costs, points, fees, reserves, carrying costs and the first phase of work before a draw.

## How to use the hard money loan calculator

1. **Total loan amount:** the full commitment, including any rehab holdback. Size it with the caps below before you enter it.
2. **Annual interest rate:** from the lender's term sheet.
3. **Origination points:** one point is 1% of the loan amount, paid at closing.
4. **Project hold time (months):** closing to payoff. Use a realistic timeline, then test a longer one.
5. **Underwriting, draw and document fees:** add every fixed fee on the term sheet: underwriting, processing, document prep, appraisal or valuation, each draw inspection, wire fees.
6. **Read the outputs:** total financing cost, the monthly interest-only payment, and upfront points plus fees.
7. **Carry the total** into the [flip profit calculator](/tools/flip-profit-calculator) or the [Maximum Purchase Price guide](/guides/maximum-purchase-price).

The calculator assumes interest accrues on the full loan amount for the whole hold. If your lender charges interest only on funds drawn, your actual interest will be lower; see the worked example.

### Leverage caps: loan-to-cost vs loan-to-ARV

Lenders protect themselves with more than one limit. Your loan is the **lowest** of them.

- **Purchase cap:** a percentage of the purchase price (or as-is value, if lower).
- **Rehab holdback:** often up to 100% of the approved rehab budget, released in draws.
- **Loan-to-cost (LTC):** the total loan divided by purchase plus rehab. Caps how much of the project you can borrow.
- **Loan-to-ARV (LTARV):** the total loan divided by the lender's after repair value. Caps the loan relative to what the house should be worth.

**Illustrative caps** you may see on term sheets (not a market survey, and they vary by lender, experience and market): purchase financing in the 80–90% range, rehab up to 100%, total LTC around 85–90%, and LTARV around 65–75%. Get current term sheets from two or three lenders.

### The draw process

Rehab money is usually held back at closing and released in stages:

1. **Scope of work and budget.** Before closing, you submit a line-item scope. The lender approves it and sets a draw schedule (for example, by phase or by line item).
2. **You fund the work first.** Most draws are reimbursements. You or your contractor pay for the work, then request the draw.
3. **Draw request.** You submit the request with photos, invoices or lien waivers, depending on the lender.
4. **Inspection.** The lender sends an inspector or accepts a photo or video inspection to confirm the work is complete.
5. **Funding.** After approval, the lender wires the draw, minus any draw fee.

**Illustrative turnaround** from request to funding is often a few business days to a week or more, depending on the lender and the inspector's schedule. Ask for the lender's typical turnaround in writing. A slow draw stalls your contractor, and a stalled contractor extends your hold.

**Draw fees** are often a flat amount per draw (illustratively, a few hundred dollars each). Fewer, larger draws cost less in fees but need more of your own cash up front.

### Borrower requirements

Hard money lenders lend mainly on the property and the plan, but they still look at the borrower. Common items:

- **Experience.** Many lenders price and size loans by the number of completed flips in a recent period. First-time borrowers often get lower leverage, a higher rate or more points.
- **Credit.** Many lenders check credit and have a minimum score. Requirements vary widely.
- **Liquidity and reserves.** Lenders often want proof that you can cover the down payment, closing costs, the first phase of work and several months of payments.
- **Entity.** Many lenders lend to an LLC or corporation rather than an individual, with a personal guarantee from the owners. Business-purpose loans on investment property are generally not offered for a home you will live in.
- **Income.** Often not verified the way a mortgage lender would, but some lenders ask for bank statements.

### Underwriting documents and timeline

A typical checklist (varies by lender):

- Purchase contract and any addenda
- Scope of work and rehab budget, and often contractor bids
- Entity documents (articles, operating agreement, EIN letter, good standing)
- Government ID for guarantors
- Bank statements for down payment and reserves
- Track record (addresses, purchase and sale dates and prices of past projects)
- Insurance binder (often builder's risk or vacant property coverage)
- Title commitment, and the lender's valuation (appraisal or broker price opinion)

Closings can happen quickly when your documents are ready and the valuation is ordered early. Delays usually come from missing documents, slow valuations or title issues. Send everything at once.

### Extensions

If you cannot pay off the loan at maturity, you will need an extension or a refinance. Extensions are usually at the lender's discretion and often cost a fee (for example, a point for a few months) and sometimes a higher rate. Ask before you sign:

- Is an extension available, and on what terms?
- What happens if the loan matures and is not extended? Is there a default rate?
- Is there a prepayment penalty or a minimum interest period if you sell early?

## Formula

```
Points                 = Loan amount × Points %
Upfront cost           = Points + Fees
Monthly interest       = Loan amount × Annual rate ÷ 12
Total interest         = Monthly interest × Months
Total financing cost   = Points + Fees + Total interest

Loan size (lowest of):
  Purchase cap         = Purchase price × Purchase %
                       + Rehab holdback
  LTC cap              = (Purchase + Rehab) × LTC %
  LTARV cap            = Lender's ARV × LTARV %
```

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar's sample deal: purchase $145,000, rehab $70,000, ARV $295,000, six-month hold. All terms are hypothetical.

**Step 1: Size the loan (hypothetical caps: 85% of purchase, 100% of rehab, 90% LTC, 70% LTARV)**

| Cap | Calculation | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase + rehab | $145,000 × 85% + $70,000 | $193,250 |
| LTC | ($145,000 + $70,000) × 90% | $193,500 |
| LTARV | $295,000 × 70% | $206,500 |
| **Loan (lowest)** | | **$193,250** |

Check: LTC = $193,250 ÷ $215,000 ≈ 89.9%. LTARV = $193,250 ÷ $295,000 ≈ 65.5%.

**Step 2: Calculator inputs**

- Loan amount: $193,250
- Rate: 11%
- Points: 2
- Months: 6
- Fees: $2,750 (underwriting and documents $1,750, plus four draw inspections at $250)

**Calculator output (interest on the full loan for six months):**

- Points: $3,865
- Upfront points + fees: $6,615
- Monthly interest-only payment: about $1,771
- Total interest: about $10,628
- **Total financing cost: about $17,244**

**Step 3: Same loan, interest charged only on funds drawn**

The $123,250 purchase portion accrues interest for six months: about $6,779. The $70,000 rehab holdback is released in four $17,500 draws at the end of months 1 through 4, so each draw accrues interest only for the months after it is released: about $2,246 in total.

| | Interest on full loan | Interest on drawn funds |
|---|---|---|
| Interest | about $10,628 | about $9,025 |
| Points and fees | $6,615 | $6,615 |
| **Total cost** | **about $17,244** | **about $15,640** |

Same rate, same points, about $1,600 different. Ask which method the lender uses, and use the matching number.

**Step 4: Cash you need (hypothetical)**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Down payment (15% of purchase) | $21,750 |
| Buying closing costs (1.5%) | $2,175 |
| Points and closing-day fees | $5,615 |

The down payment is only about half of the cash needed to get the job moving.

**Step 5: the lender's appraiser says $265,000.** The LTARV cap drops to $185,500 ($265,000 × 70%), so the loan shrinks by $7,750 and you bring that much more cash. A conservative ARV protects you here too.

## What the result means

- **Total financing cost** is a real cost of the flip. Add it to your profit model; do not treat it as overhead.
- **Monthly interest-only payment** is cash you need every month during the hold, unless your lender defers interest to payoff.
- **Upfront points and fees** are sunk at closing. Selling early saves interest, not points.
- **Comparing lenders:** compare total cost at your realistic hold **and** at your long-hold scenario. A lower rate with more points can be cheaper on a long hold and more expensive on a short one.

## Assumptions

- Simple interest, interest-only payments.
- Interest accrues on the full loan for the whole hold (the calculator's method).
- No extension, default interest, exit fee or prepayment penalty.
- Fees are entered as a single total.

## Limitations

- **No draw schedule.** The calculator cannot model interest on drawn funds only. Use the hand method in the example.
- **No loan sizing.** You need to apply the purchase, LTC and LTARV caps yourself.
- **No extension modeling.** Re-run with more months and add the extension fee.
- **Not a quote.** Only a lender's term sheet and loan documents set your terms.
- **Not legal advice.** Read the note, the deed of trust or mortgage, the guaranty and the construction loan agreement before you sign.

## Common mistakes

1. **Comparing rates without comparing points, fees and interest method.**
2. **Assuming the lender will lend on your ARV.** It lends on its own valuation.
3. **Forgetting you fund each phase before the draw.**
4. **Using the optimistic hold.** Price the long one, including an extension fee.
5. **Ignoring prepayment terms and minimum interest.** Some loans charge a minimum number of months of interest.
6. **Missing the personal guarantee.** An LLC does not keep you off the hook if you sign a guaranty.
7. **Sending documents piecemeal.** It slows underwriting and can cost you the contract deadline.
8. **Not reading the draw rules.** Retainage, inspection method and turnaround all affect your schedule.

## FAQ

### How do hard money loan points work?

Each point is 1% of the loan amount, usually paid at closing. Two points on a $193,250 loan is $3,865. Ask whether points apply to the full commitment, including the rehab holdback, or only to the initial advance.

### Do hard money loans have interest-only payments?

Many do, with the principal due at payoff. Some lenders defer interest to payoff. Confirm the payment schedule and whether interest is charged on the full loan or only on funds drawn.

### What is the difference between loan-to-cost and loan-to-ARV?

Loan-to-cost compares the loan to your purchase price plus rehab. Loan-to-ARV compares it to the after repair value. Lenders apply both, and your loan is limited by whichever is lower.

### How do rehab draws work?

The lender holds back the rehab budget at closing. As work is completed, you request a draw, the lender inspects, and then it funds the draw, usually as a reimbursement. You need cash to fund each phase before the money arrives.

### How long does a draw take to fund?

It varies by lender and inspector availability. Illustratively, a few business days to a week or more. Ask for the lender's typical turnaround before you sign.

### What do hard money lenders require from borrowers?

Common items include an entity such as an LLC, a personal guarantee, a credit check, proof of funds and reserves, a scope of work and budget, and a track record. First-time borrowers often get less leverage or higher pricing.

### Do I need an LLC for a hard money loan?

Many lenders require one for business-purpose loans, with the owners personally guaranteeing the loan. Check with the lender, and talk to an attorney about how you set up the entity.

### What happens if I need more time?

You may be able to extend, often for a fee and sometimes a higher rate, at the lender's discretion. If you cannot extend or refinance, you are in default. Plan your exit inside the original term.

### Can I roll points and fees into the loan?

Some lenders allow it. You then pay interest on a larger balance, and the loan may hit a leverage cap sooner. Compare total cost both ways.

### Is hard money better than private money for a first flip?

Each has trade-offs in cost, speed, flexibility and relationship risk. See [hard money vs private money for a first flip](/guides/hard-money-vs-private-money-for-a-first-flip).
