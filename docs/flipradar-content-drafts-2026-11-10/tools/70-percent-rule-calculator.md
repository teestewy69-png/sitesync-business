The 70% rule is a screening shortcut: pay no more than 70% of the after repair value, minus the repairs. It exists to stop you from overpaying before you have done the full math. It is not a profit formula, and the 70% is not sacred.

This calculator gives you a maximum allowable offer (MAO) from three inputs, shows what the leftover margin has to cover, and lets you set your own percentage. Free, no account, nothing to buy.

## Key takeaways

- **MAO = (ARV × rule %) − repairs.** The output is a ceiling, not a target.
- **The 30% is not your profit.** It has to cover buying costs, holding costs, financing, selling costs **and** profit. On FlipRadar's sample deal, more than 14% of ARV goes to costs before any profit.
- **The rule does not include closing costs as a separate line.** They live inside the 30%. Your repairs input must be the full rehab budget.
- **Use the percentage as an output of your own costs**, not a dial you turn to win a deal. A worked method is below.
- **In a hot market, tighten the percentage. In a slower market, loosen it only if the full numbers still protect your margin.** Competition makes overpaying easy, and stretched values are where flips get hurt.
- **A negative MAO means no price works** for a flip at those numbers.
- **Wholesalers subtract their fee from the end buyer's MAO,** not from the ARV.

## How to use the 70% rule calculator

1. **Enter the ARV.** Use the conservative figure from your comps, not the top comp. The [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator) shows how to build one with a confidence range.
2. **Enter the estimated rehab.** Use your full budget including contingency, permits, dumpsters and cleaning. The [rehab cost estimator](/tools/rehab-cost-estimator) builds one by category.
3. **Enter the rule percentage.** Start at 70%, then run 65% and 75% to see the spread. Better: compute your own percentage using the method below.
4. **Read the three outputs:**
   - **Maximum allowable offer:** ARV × % − rehab.
   - **Gross ARV ceiling:** ARV × %, the most your purchase and rehab together can cost.
   - **Gross margin buffer:** ARV × (100% − rule %), the dollars left to cover every other cost and your profit.
5. **Compare the MAO to the asking price.** If the asking price is above your MAO, either negotiate, change the plan, or pass.
6. **Confirm with full numbers** in the [flip profit calculator](/tools/flip-profit-calculator) or the [Maximum Purchase Price guide](/guides/maximum-purchase-price) before you sign.

## Inputs explained

| Input | What it means | How to get a defensible number |
|---|---|---|
| After repair value (ARV) | What the house should sell for after your planned rehab | Three or more adjusted, sold, renovated comps. Underwrite at or below the middle of the range |
| Estimated rehab / repairs | Everything it costs to deliver the house in ARV condition | Room-by-room scope with local bids or your price list, plus contingency |
| Rule percentage | The share of ARV your purchase plus rehab can consume | Start at 70%, then replace it with your own percentage built from your costs and profit target |

### Wholesale or assignment fee

If you are a wholesaler, your buyer's MAO is the ceiling for **your buyer's total price**, including your fee:

```
Wholesaler's max contract price = End buyer's MAO − Assignment fee
```

Example: if a flipper's MAO is $136,500 and your fee is $10,000, your contract price with the seller can be no more than $126,500. If you contract at $136,500 and add your fee, the deal no longer works for the buyer, and you will not sell the contract. Many flippers apply a tighter percentage to wholesale deals because they are taking on an inspection and scope they did not control.

## Formula

```
Gross ARV ceiling       = ARV × Rule %
Maximum allowable offer = (ARV × Rule %) − Rehab
Gross margin buffer     = ARV × (1 − Rule %)
Wholesale contract max  = MAO − Assignment fee
```

### How to set your own percentage

The 70% rule is a stand-in for a real calculation. You can do the real calculation once for your typical deal and turn it into a percentage:

```
Your rule % = 1 − (Selling costs % + Buying costs % + Holding and financing % + Target profit %)
              (each expressed as a share of ARV)
```

Using FlipRadar's sample deal (hypothetical): selling costs 7.0% of ARV, buying costs about 0.7%, holding and financing about 6.7%, and a target profit of 12% of ARV:

```
Your rule % = 1 − (7.0% + 0.7% + 6.7% + 12%) = 1 − 26.4% ≈ 73.6%
```

At that price point, with those costs and that profit target, a percentage near 73–74% is defensible. Change the financing, the hold time, the selling costs or the price point and the right percentage changes. Re-run it whenever your costs or market change.

**Why a fixed percentage misleads at the extremes:** many costs do not scale with price. A $120,000 house still needs a title policy, insurance, a lender's fees and months of carrying costs. At the bottom of the market, 70% can leave too few dollars of profit for the risk. At the top, 70% can leave a large dollar cushion that you may not need to win the deal. The percentage is a shortcut. The dollars are what you live on.

### Hot vs slow markets: FlipRadar's rule

- **Hot market: tighten the percentage** (a lower % of ARV, which lowers your max offer). When buyers compete, it is easy to overpay, comps may already reflect stretched prices, and a cooling market during your four-to-six-month hold hits you at the sale. You need more cushion, not less.
- **Slower market: you may loosen the percentage** (a higher %) **only if the deal and the exit still protect your margin.** Less competition can mean better terms and a more realistic ARV, but also longer days on market. Price in the longer hold before you move the percentage.

Example ranges some investors use are around 60–65% in a heated market and up to 75% in a slower one. These are examples, not rules. Plenty of other guidance says the opposite: raise the percentage in a hot market to win deals. FlipRadar disagrees. Raising your percentage in a hot market means paying more for a house at the moment values are most likely to be stretched, with less room for error.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar's sample property: a 1,500 sq ft, 3 bed / 2 bath ranch. All numbers are hypothetical.

- ARV: **$295,000** (conservative figure from three adjusted comps; see the [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator) example)
- Rehab: **$70,000** ($62,500 scope plus a 12% contingency; see the [rehab cost estimator](/tools/rehab-cost-estimator) example)
- Asking price: **$185,000**

### 65%, 70% and 75% side by side

| | 65% | 70% | 75% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross ARV ceiling (ARV × %) | $191,750 | $206,500 | $221,250 |
| Minus rehab | −$70,000 | −$70,000 | −$70,000 |
| **Maximum allowable offer** | **$121,750** | **$136,500** | **$151,250** |
| Gross margin buffer | $103,250 | $88,500 | $73,750 |
| Asking price $185,000 vs MAO | $63,250 over | $48,500 over | $33,750 over |

At every percentage, the asking price fails. The seller is pricing the house as if it needed far less work.

### Sensitivity: ARV and rehab both move

MAO at 70% when your ARV or rehab estimate is off:

| ARV ↓ / Rehab → | $60,000 | $70,000 | $85,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $280,000 (ARV 5% low) | $136,000 | $126,000 | $111,000 |
| $295,000 (base) | $146,500 | **$136,500** | $121,500 |
| $310,000 (ARV 5% high) | $157,000 | $147,000 | $132,000 |

Every dollar of rehab overrun comes straight off your MAO, and every 5% of ARV error moves the MAO by about $10,000 on this house. If the deal only works in the top-left corner (higher ARV, lower rehab), it is a hope, not a deal.

### What the 30% has to cover in this example

If you bought at the 70% MAO of $136,500, here is what the $88,500 buffer has to pay for (hypothetical costs: 1.5% buying costs, a hard money loan at 11% with 2 points and $2,750 in fees, $700 a month in taxes, insurance and utilities for six months, and 7% selling costs):

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Buying closing costs | about $2,050 |
| Financing (points, fees, interest) | about $15,100 |
| Holding (taxes, insurance, utilities, 6 months) | $4,200 |
| Selling costs (7% of ARV) | $20,650 |
| **Total costs inside the 30%** | **about $42,000** |
| **What is left as profit (before tax)** | **about $46,500** |

Roughly half the "30%" goes to costs before you earn anything. At the $185,000 asking price, the same costs turn the deal into a loss of about $5,800.

## What the result means

- **Asking price below your MAO:** the deal passes the first screen. Now verify the ARV, get bids, and run the full profit math.
- **Asking price above your MAO:** do not stretch the percentage to make it fit. Negotiate with your numbers, change the scope, or pass.
- **MAO close to the asking price:** treat it as a fail unless the full calculation shows your target profit after a longer hold and a lower ARV.
- **Negative MAO:** rehab is larger than ARV × %. No purchase price works as a flip at these numbers. The property may still have value to a builder, a buy-and-hold investor, or as land, but not to you as a flip at this scope.
- **Gross margin buffer:** a quick reality check. If it is smaller than your expected selling, holding and financing costs plus your profit target, the percentage is too high for this deal.

### How different investors use the MAO

- **First-time flipper:** use a tighter percentage than you think you need. Your rehab estimate and timeline are the least proven numbers in the deal, and the percentage is your only protection against both.
- **Experienced flipper:** replace the 70% with your own percentage from past deals, then confirm with a full Maximum Purchase Price calculation on anything that gets to contract.
- **Wholesaler:** work backward from your buyers' MAO, subtract your fee, and leave room for the buyer's own inspection findings.
- **Agent advising an investor client:** the MAO is a useful way to explain to a seller why an investor's offer is below list. Show the math, not just the number.

## Assumptions

- ARV and rehab are honest, conservative estimates.
- The rule percentage reflects your typical buying, holding, financing and selling costs and your profit target.
- The hold is a typical one for your market. Longer holds need a lower percentage.
- Financing costs are typical for you. A deal financed with expensive short-term money needs more room than an all-cash deal.
- Selling uses your usual method (agent, concessions, transfer taxes).

## Limitations

- **One percentage hides many costs.** It does not show interest, points, transfer taxes, concessions or holding time separately.
- **No time dimension.** The rule does not change if the project takes four months or ten.
- **Bad at the price extremes**, as shown above.
- **Not built for rentals.** BRRRR and buy-and-hold deals need rent, debt service and refinance math. Use the [BRRRR calculator](/tools/brrrr-calculator).
- **Not a substitute for full underwriting** or for a lender's appraisal and approval.

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating the 30% as profit.** It is the budget for every non-rehab cost and your profit.
2. **Subtracting closing costs twice.** If your percentage already accounts for closing costs, do not subtract them again from the MAO. If you built your own percentage without them, do.
3. **Leaving costs out of the rehab input.** Permits, dumpsters, final cleaning, appliances and contingency belong in rehab.
4. **Raising the percentage to win a bidding war.** That is how investors buy at the top.
5. **Using the top comp as ARV.** The MAO inherits every dollar of ARV optimism.
6. **Adding your wholesale fee on top of the buyer's MAO.** Subtract it.
7. **Applying the rule to a rental.** Rentals are judged on cash flow and refinance math.
8. **Never updating the percentage.** When rates, insurance or selling costs change, so does your number.

## FAQ

### What is the 70% rule in house flipping?

A screening rule that says a flipper should pay no more than 70% of a property's after repair value, minus repair costs. The remaining 30% of ARV is meant to cover buying, holding, financing and selling costs plus profit.

### Is the 30% all profit?

No. The 30% has to pay for closing costs on both ends, loan interest and points, taxes, insurance, utilities and selling costs. On FlipRadar's sample deal, those costs take about 14% of ARV, leaving about 16% as profit at the 70% MAO.

### Does the 70% rule include closing costs?

Yes, implicitly. Buying and selling closing costs are meant to come out of the 30% buffer, not to be subtracted separately. Your repairs input should still include every rehab cost.

### Should I use 65%, 70% or 75%?

Use the percentage your own costs and profit target produce. As a starting point, tighten toward 65% or lower in a hot market or on a riskier scope, and only move toward 75% in a slower market when the full numbers still show your target profit.

### Why tighten the percentage in a hot market?

Because competition makes overpaying easy and comps may already reflect stretched prices. If the market cools during your hold, the sale price is where you take the loss. A tighter percentage buys you cushion when cushion is most likely to be needed.

### Is the 70% rule still accurate with higher interest rates?

The rule never knew what interest rate you pay. Higher rates, points and longer holds all raise financing costs inside the 30%, which shrinks profit. Recalculate your own percentage when your financing terms change.

### What does a negative MAO mean?

It means the repairs are larger than the gross ceiling (ARV × your percentage). No purchase price makes the flip work at those numbers. Reduce scope only if the ARV still holds, or pass.

### How do wholesalers use the 70% rule?

A wholesaler takes the end buyer's MAO and subtracts the assignment fee to find the highest contract price they can sign with the seller. Many end buyers use a tighter percentage on wholesale deals.

### Can I use the 70% rule for BRRRR or rentals?

Only as a rough screen on the purchase. A rental succeeds or fails on rent, expenses, debt service and how much cash the refinance returns. Use the [BRRRR calculator](/tools/brrrr-calculator) or [how to analyze a BRRRR deal](/guides/how-to-analyze-a-brrrr-deal).

### Is the 70% rule a law or a lender requirement?

Neither. It is an investor rule of thumb. Lenders have their own limits, usually expressed as a maximum loan-to-cost and loan-to-ARV. See the [hard money loan calculator](/tools/hard-money-loan-calculator) for how those caps work.
