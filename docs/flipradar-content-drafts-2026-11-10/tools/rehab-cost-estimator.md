Rehab is the number most first flips get wrong, and it is wrong in one direction: too low. The house you walk through is not the house you will open up. This estimator helps you build a budget in the categories that actually drive cost, adds a contingency, and gives you a cost per square foot to sanity-check against your own bids.

It works only with the numbers you enter. FlipRadar does not know your local labor rates, and no national average does either. Free, no account, nothing to buy.

## Key takeaways

- **Scope first, dollars second.** Write down what you will do in each room before you price anything.
- **Kitchens, baths and big systems** (roof, HVAC, electrical, plumbing, foundation) decide most budgets. Paint and flooring rarely sink a deal. Systems do.
- **Cost per square foot is a check, not a method.** Two houses of the same size can need very different work.
- **Contingency is a line item, not a hope.** Size it to the age and condition of the house.
- **Your own price list beats any table,** including the illustrative ones on this page. Build it from local bids and supplier pricing, then update it after every job.
- **Time is a rehab cost.** A scope that adds two months adds two months of interest, taxes, insurance and utilities.

## How to use the rehab cost estimator

1. **Walk the house with a written scope.** Room by room, write what stays, what is repaired and what is replaced. Note anything you cannot see: inside walls, under floors, the sewer line, the attic.
2. **Enter the home's square footage** (finished living area).
3. **Enter the kitchen budget.** Cabinets, counters, sink and faucet, backsplash, appliances, lighting, and the labor to install them.
4. **Enter the total for all bathrooms.** Price each bath separately, then add them together.
5. **Enter roof, HVAC and mechanicals.** Roof, HVAC, electrical, plumbing, water heater, and any structural or foundation work.
6. **Enter flooring, paint and fixtures as a cost per square foot.** This covers finishes that scale with size: flooring, interior paint, doors and trim, light fixtures, hardware, plus smaller items like landscaping, permits, dumpsters and final cleaning if you choose to spread them across the square footage.
7. **Set your contingency percentage.** See the guidance below.
8. **Read the outputs:** total budget with contingency, cost per square foot, base scope, the cosmetic portion, and the contingency reserve.
9. **Compare the total to real bids** before you make an offer, and carry it into the [70% rule calculator](/tools/70-percent-rule-calculator).

## Inputs explained

| Input | What belongs here | Common misses |
|---|---|---|
| Home square footage | Finished living area | Using lot size or including unfinished space |
| Kitchen budget | Cabinets, counters, sink, faucet, backsplash, appliances, lighting, install labor, minor drywall and electrical in the kitchen | Appliances, delivery and haul-away, a range hood vent, under-cabinet lighting |
| Bathrooms total | Each bath: vanity, toilet, tub or shower, tile, fixtures, exhaust fan, mirror, lighting | Subfloor rot around toilets and tubs, waterproofing behind tile, valve replacement |
| Roof / HVAC / mechanicals | Roof, HVAC, electrical panel and wiring, plumbing, water heater, sewer, structural work | Sewer line repairs, panel upgrades required by the inspector, permit-triggered code upgrades |
| Flooring, paint and fixtures ($/sq ft) | Flooring, interior paint, doors, trim, light fixtures, hardware, smoke and CO detectors. Optionally: landscaping, permits, dumpsters, final clean | Transitions, baseboards, stair treads, ceiling paint, closets |
| Contingency (%) | A reserve for what you cannot see yet | Setting it at zero because the house "looks fine" |

### Contingency: how much

These are FlipRadar's starting points, not rules:

| House and scope | Contingency to start with |
|---|---|
| Newer house, cosmetic scope, systems inspected and fine | About 10% |
| 1960s–1980s house, mid-level rehab, some systems unknown | About 12–15% |
| Older house, full gut, layout changes, or anything with known water, structural or sewer issues | 15–20% or more |

If an inspection or a contractor's walk-through has already priced the unknowns, you can carry a smaller contingency. If you have not had anyone qualified look at the roof, sewer, electrical and foundation, carry more.

### Illustrative cost per square foot by scope

**Illustrative only.** Use these to catch obvious errors, not to set a budget.

| Scope | What it usually includes | Illustrative range per sq ft of living area |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic | Paint, flooring, fixtures, hardware, light kitchen and bath refresh, landscaping | $10–$30 |
| Mid-level | New kitchen, one or two bath remodels, flooring and paint throughout, some systems | $30–$60 |
| Full gut | Studs-out, new systems, new kitchen and baths, possible layout changes | $60–$120+ |

If your cost per square foot falls outside the range for the scope you selected, one of two things is true: your market is unusual, or your scope and your budget do not match. Find out which.

### Regional differences: how to adjust

FlipRadar does not publish a regional cost index because we have not verified one that holds at the street level. What you can rely on is the direction of the main drivers:

| Driver | Pushes costs up | Pushes costs down |
|---|---|---|
| Labor market | High-wage metros, tight trades, union rules | Lower-wage areas, many available crews |
| Permits and code | Strict local code, long plan review, required upgrades on permit | Simple over-the-counter permits |
| Climate | Freeze protection, snow loads, hurricane or wildfire requirements | Mild climates |
| Housing stock | Older homes (plaster, galvanized pipe, old wiring) | Newer construction |
| Access | Dense cities with parking, staging and disposal limits | Suburbs with driveways and easy dumpster placement |

To build your own regional factor, price the same scope (the same kitchen, the same bath, the same square footage of flooring) with two or three local contractors and compare it to your price list. That ratio is your local adjustment until your next set of bids.

### Building your own price list

A price list (some investors call it a rehab menu) is the single biggest upgrade to your estimates. It turns "the kitchen will be about $20,000" into "10 base cabinets at $X, 32 sq ft of counter at $Y, install at $Z."

1. **Pick your standard finish spec.** Same flooring, same paint, same fixtures, same cabinet line on every flip at a given price point. Fewer choices, faster estimates, fewer mistakes.
2. **Price materials** at the suppliers you will actually use, including delivery.
3. **Get unit labor prices** from two or three contractors per trade: per square foot of flooring installed, per door hung, per fixture swapped, per square of roofing.
4. **Write it down by unit:** each, square foot, linear foot, square, opening.
5. **Add the hidden lines:** permits, dumpsters, portable toilet, final clean, utility deposits, lockbox, insurance.
6. **Update after every job.** Compare estimated vs actual, line by line. The gaps teach you more than any article.

## Formula

```
Cosmetic scope   = Flooring/paint/fixtures $/sq ft × Square footage
Base scope       = Kitchen + Bathrooms + Roof/HVAC/Mechanicals + Cosmetic scope
Contingency      = Base scope × Contingency %
Total budget     = Base scope + Contingency
Cost per sq ft   = Total budget ÷ Square footage
```

**Calculator inputs for the same budget:** square footage 1,500; kitchen $16,500; bathrooms $13,000; roof/HVAC/mechanicals $14,900 (roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC); flooring, paint and fixtures $12 per sq ft (the remaining $18,100 spread over 1,500 sq ft); contingency 12%. The calculator returns about $69,900, close to the line-item total.

**The decision hidden in this budget:** the HVAC is 18 years old and working. Replacing it might cost $8,000–$12,000 (illustrative). Keeping it saves that money but leaves a risk: a buyer's inspector may flag its age, and a failure during the hold comes out of contingency. Two reasonable choices: keep it and hold enough contingency to replace it, or replace it and market the new system. What you should not do is keep it **and** cut contingency.

## What the result means

- **Total budget:** the number to put into your MAO and profit calculations. Use the total with contingency, not the base.
- **Cost per square foot:** compare it with the illustrative scope bands and with your past jobs. A mid-level scope coming out at $20 per sq ft is usually missing something.
- **Cosmetic portion:** if most of your budget is cosmetic on an older house, check whether you have actually inspected the systems.
- **Contingency reserve:** money you plan to spend. If you finish without touching it, treat it as a bonus, not as a reason to cut it next time.

### Cosmetic flip vs full gut: which one

| Choose a cosmetic flip when | Choose a heavier rehab when |
|---|---|
| Systems are inspected and in good shape | Systems are near end of life or failed inspection |
| Renovated comps in the area are lightly updated | Comps are fully renovated and buyers expect it |
| You want a shorter hold and less permit exposure | The spread between as-is price and ARV justifies the risk |
| You are new and want to limit unknowns | You have crews, a price list and contingency you trust |

The most expensive part of most rehabs is the kitchen, followed by bathrooms and major systems. The most expensive **mistake** is usually a system you assumed was fine.

### How long the rehab takes

These are illustrative ranges for work time, not including permit lead time, materials on backorder, or time on the market:

| Scope | Illustrative work time |
|---|---|
| Cosmetic | About 2–6 weeks |
| Mid-level | About 6–12 weeks |
| Full gut | About 3–6 months or longer |

Permit review, inspection scheduling and long-lead items (windows, cabinets, electrical gear) can add weeks. Ask your local building department for current review times before you set your schedule. Then add listing and closing time to get your full hold for the [holding costs guide](/guides/holding-costs-on-a-6-month-flip).

## Assumptions

- You entered the real scope of work, not the scope you hope will be enough.
- Prices are local and current, ideally from bids.
- The contingency covers unknowns, not scope you already know about.
- Permits, dumpsters and cleaning are included somewhere in the inputs.
- The house will be finished to the level of your ARV comps.

## Limitations

- **Five buckets, not a full scope.** The tool does not itemize every line or know what your contractor will find.
- **No local pricing.** Every dollar comes from you.
- **No timeline.** The tool does not calculate holding cost from rehab duration. Use the [flip profit calculator](/tools/flip-profit-calculator) for that.
- **Not a bid.** Lenders typically want a line-item scope of work and often a contractor's bid. The estimator helps you build it, but it is not a substitute.
- **Not an inspection.** It cannot see the sewer line, the wiring inside the walls or the subfloor under the tub.

## Common mistakes

1. **Pricing from photos.** Walk the house. Better, walk it with a contractor.
2. **Skipping the sewer scope and roof check** on an older house. Both are inexpensive to inspect and expensive to discover late.
3. **Using national averages.** They are averages of markets that are not yours.
4. **Forgetting the small lines:** permits, dumpsters, final cleaning, utility turn-on, lockbox, appliances, delivery.
5. **No contingency, or contingency spent on known scope.** If you already know about it, it is scope.
6. **Over-improving.** Finishes above your comps rarely pay back. Match the comps.
7. **Ignoring permit-triggered upgrades.** Opening walls or replacing a panel can require code upgrades elsewhere.
8. **Not separating labor and materials.** You cannot negotiate or check what you cannot see.
9. **Treating the budget as fixed after closing.** Re-forecast weekly against actuals.

## FAQ

### How much does it cost to rehab a house for a flip?

It depends on scope, size, age and local labor, so any single number is misleading. As an illustrative starting point, cosmetic rehabs often fall in the low tens of dollars per square foot, mid-level rehabs higher, and full guts much higher. Build your own budget from a room-by-room scope and local bids.

### What is the most expensive part of a rehab?

Usually the kitchen, then bathrooms and major systems like the roof, HVAC, electrical and plumbing. Foundation and sewer problems can outrun all of them, which is why they are worth inspecting before you buy.

### How much contingency should I carry?

FlipRadar's starting points are about 10% for a newer house with a cosmetic scope, 12–15% for an older house with a mid-level scope, and 15–20% or more for full guts or known water, structural or sewer issues.

### Is cost per square foot a good way to estimate rehab?

As a check, yes. As a method, no. Two 1,500 sq ft houses can need $20,000 or $120,000 of work. Price the scope, then use cost per square foot to catch errors.

### How long does a flip rehab take?

Illustratively, a cosmetic rehab might take a few weeks, a mid-level rehab two to three months, and a full gut several months, before permit lead times and time on the market. Your contractors and building department set the real schedule.

### Should I do a cosmetic flip or a full rehab?

Match the comps and the systems. If renovated comps are lightly updated and the systems are sound, a cosmetic flip keeps the hold short and the risk low. If buyers in that area expect a full renovation, or systems are failing, budget for it or pass.

### How do I build a rehab price list?

Standardize your finishes, price materials at your suppliers, get unit labor prices from two or three contractors per trade, record everything by unit, and update it after every job by comparing estimated with actual costs.

### Do I need permits for a flip rehab?

Often, yes, for electrical, plumbing, structural, roofing, HVAC and window work, depending on your jurisdiction. Cosmetic work like paint usually does not need one. Call the local building department before you set your budget and schedule.

### Can I use this estimate for a loan application?

Use it to organize your scope and numbers. Most rehab lenders want a line-item scope of work and often a contractor's bid, and they release rehab money in draws as work is completed. See the [hard money loan calculator](/tools/hard-money-loan-calculator) for how draws work.

### What rehab costs do investors underestimate most?

Systems behind walls and under floors, permit-triggered code upgrades, and time. See [rehab budget line items most flippers forget](/guides/rehab-budget-line-items-most-flippers-forget).
