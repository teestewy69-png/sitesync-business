BRRRR stands for buy, rehab, rent, refinance, repeat. You buy a house that needs work, renovate it, rent it, refinance it on the higher value, and use the cash that comes back to do it again. When it works, you end up owning a rental with little of your own money left in it. When it does not, you own a rental with more of your money stuck in it than you planned, or a rental that costs you money every month.

This calculator shows the three numbers that decide which one you get: cash left in the deal after the refinance, monthly cash flow, and cash-on-cash return. This page adds what lenders check that the calculator does not: DSCR, seasoning, and how the refinance is sized. Free, no account, nothing to buy.

## Key takeaways

- **Cash left in the deal = all-in cost − new loan.** That is the money the refinance did not give back.
- **Capital recovery** is the share of your cash the refinance returned. 100% or more means "nothing left in," which some investors call an infinite return. It also means the property carries the maximum debt.
- **The refinance is sized by the lender's appraisal, its maximum LTV, and its DSCR test,** whichever is lowest. Plan for all three.
- **A DSCR around 1.20–1.25 is a common underwriting rule of thumb** for rental loans. It is not a promise; each lender sets its own minimums.
- **Passing the lender's DSCR does not mean the rental cash flows.** Lender DSCR typically ignores vacancy, management, maintenance and capital repairs. You cannot.
- **Seasoning rules can change your timeline.** Under Fannie Mae's guide, paying off a first mortgage through a cash-out refinance generally requires that mortgage to be at least 12 months old. A short-term rehab loan may not fit.
- **Refinance cash is a loan, not income.** It is generally not taxed as income, but confirm your situation with a CPA.

## How to use the BRRRR calculator

1. **Purchase price:** the contract price.
2. **Rehab cost:** the full rehab budget. The calculator's all-in cost is purchase plus rehab, so **add buying closing costs, holding costs, rehab-loan interest, points and fees, and expected refinance closing costs to this field** if you want a true cash-left number.
3. **After repair value (ARV):** what an appraiser should value the finished, rented property at. Use conservative, sold comps. See the [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator).
4. **Refinance LTV (%):** the lender's maximum loan-to-value for a cash-out refinance on a rental.
5. **Refinance interest rate (30-year %):** from a current quote.
6. **Projected monthly rent:** market rent for the finished property, from comparable leased rentals, not asking rents.
7. **Taxes, insurance, vacancy and management (% of rent):** include property taxes, insurance, vacancy, management, maintenance and capital reserves. See the worked example for how to build it.
8. **Read the outputs:** cash left in the deal, estimated monthly net cash flow, and projected cash-on-cash return (shown as "infinite" when no cash is left).
9. **Then check DSCR and seasoning by hand** using the formulas below.

## Inputs explained

| Input | What it means | Where to get it | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | Contract price | Your contract | Using asking price |
| Rehab cost | Full rehab budget (plus other costs, see step 2) | Your scope and bids | Leaving out holding and financing |
| ARV | Appraised value after rehab | Adjusted sold comps | Assuming the appraiser will agree with your top comp |
| Refinance LTV | Max loan as % of appraised value | Lender quote | Assuming the highest LTV you have seen advertised |
| Refinance rate | 30-year rate on the new loan | Lender quote | Using an owner-occupant rate for a rental loan |
| Monthly rent | Market rent after rehab | Leased comps, local property managers | Using asking rents or your best unit |
| Operating % of rent | Taxes, insurance, vacancy, management, maintenance, capital reserves | Tax bill, insurance quote, local manager | Leaving out maintenance and capital reserves |

## Formula

```
All-in cost          = Purchase + Rehab (+ closing, holding, financing, refi costs if added)
New loan             = ARV × Refinance LTV
Cash left in deal    = All-in cost − New loan        (zero or less = all cash returned)
Monthly P&I          = Standard 30-year payment on the new loan at the refinance rate
Operating costs      = Monthly rent × Operating %
Monthly cash flow    = Rent − Operating costs − P&I
Cash-on-cash return  = (Monthly cash flow × 12) ÷ Cash left in deal

By hand:
Capital recovery %   = Cash returned at refinance ÷ Cash you invested before refinance
Lender-style DSCR    = Monthly rent ÷ PITIA (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, HOA)
Investor-style DSCR  = (Rent − all operating costs) ÷ Principal and interest
```

Lenders define DSCR in different ways. Many rental-loan (DSCR) lenders use gross rent divided by the full monthly housing payment (PITIA). Commercial lenders often use net operating income divided by debt service. Ask which one your lender uses.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar's sample rental. All numbers are hypothetical.

**The house:** 3 bed / 1.5 bath, 1,200 sq ft. Purchase $85,000, rehab $40,000, ARV $185,000, market rent $1,950 a month.

**Phase 1: buy and rehab (six months to refinance)**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase | $85,000 |
| Rehab | $40,000 |
| Buying closing costs (1.5%) | $1,275 |
| Hard money: 85% of purchase + 100% of rehab = $112,250 loan; 11%, 2 points, $2,500 fees; interest on drawn funds | $10,002 (points $2,245, fees $2,500, interest $5,257) |
| Taxes, insurance, utilities while vacant ($550 a month × 6) | $3,300 |
| **All-in before refinance** | **$139,577** |
| Your cash in (down payment, closing, points, fees, interest, carrying costs) | about $27,300 |

**Phase 2: refinance (hypothetical DSCR loan at 75% LTV, 7.25%, 30 years, closing costs 2.5% of the new loan)**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Appraised value | $185,000 |
| New loan (75%) | $138,750 |
| Pays off the rehab loan | −$112,250 |
| Refinance closing costs | −$3,469 |
| **Cash back to you** | **about $23,000** |
| **Cash left in the deal** | **about $4,300** |
| **Capital recovery** | **about 84%** |

**Calculator inputs for the same deal:** purchase $85,000; rehab $58,046 (the $40,000 rehab plus $18,046 of closing, holding, financing and refinance costs); ARV $185,000; LTV 75%; rate 7.25%; rent $1,950; operating 44.4% of rent.

**Phase 3: rent**

| Monthly | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent | $1,950 |
| Property taxes and insurance | −$300 |
| Vacancy (8%) | −$156 |
| Management (9%) | −$176 |
| Maintenance (6%) | −$117 |
| Capital reserves (6%) | −$117 |
| Operating costs (44.4% of rent) | −$866 |
| Principal and interest | −$947 |
| **Monthly cash flow** | **about $138** |
| Annual cash flow | about $1,650 |
| Cash-on-cash on $4,300 left in | about 38% |

**DSCR checks:**

- Lender-style: $1,950 ÷ ($947 + $300) ≈ **1.56**. Comfortably above a 1.20–1.25 rule of thumb.
- Investor-style: ($1,950 − $866) ÷ $947 ≈ **1.14**. Much thinner.

The deal returns most of the cash and passes the lender test, but the real cash flow is about $138 a month. One water heater or one month of vacancy takes a year of cash flow. That is not a reason to reject it, but it is the honest picture.

### Sensitivity

| Scenario | Cash left in deal | Monthly cash flow | Lender DSCR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base (75% LTV, 7.25%, appraisal $185,000) | about $4,300 | about $138 | 1.56 |
| Lower LTV (70%) | about $13,300 | about $201 | 1.65 |
| Rate 8.00% instead of 7.25% | about $4,300 | about $66 | 1.48 |
| Appraisal $170,000 (75% LTV) | about $15,300 | about $215 | 1.67 |

A lower appraisal or a lower LTV leaves more money in the deal but improves cash flow, because the loan is smaller. A higher rate keeps your cash out but eats the cash flow. There is no setting that maximizes both.

## What the result means

- **Cash left in the deal at or below zero:** the refinance returned all of your cash. The property now carries the maximum debt the lender would allow, so cash flow is usually thinnest here.
- **Small cash left, positive cash flow after all expenses:** what most BRRRR investors are aiming for.
- **Large cash left:** the deal behaves more like a normal rental purchase with a down payment. That can be fine; it is just not recycling your capital.
- **Negative cash flow:** the property costs you money every month. A higher LTV makes this worse.
- **Infinite cash-on-cash:** a math artifact of dividing by zero cash left. Judge the deal on cash flow, DSCR and risk instead.

### Good BRRRR vs bad BRRRR

| Good BRRRR | Bad BRRRR |
|---|---|
| Bought far enough below ARV that a 70–75% refinance covers most of the cost | Needs an 80% LTV and a perfect appraisal to get cash back |
| Rent is strong relative to value, so DSCR passes with room | Rent barely covers the payment at the lender's test |
| Positive cash flow after vacancy, management, maintenance and reserves | "Cash flow" only exists if you leave out maintenance and vacancy |
| Rehab is durable: rental-grade finishes, systems addressed | Cosmetic-only rehab on old systems that will fail on your watch |
| Refinance lender and seasoning confirmed before purchase | Refinance plan is "we will find a lender" |

FlipRadar's flip sample (ARV $295,000, rent $2,150) is a good example of a bad BRRRR: at a 75% refinance, lender-style DSCR is about 1.14 and monthly cash flow is negative even before full reserves. It may still be a good flip. See [BRRRR vs flip](/guides/brrrr-vs-flip-which-fits-your-capital).

### BRRRR pros and cons

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Can recycle most of your cash into the next deal | Two loans, two closings, two sets of costs |
| You keep the asset, the rent and any long-term appreciation | Refinance depends on an appraisal and lender rules you do not control |
| Buying below value creates equity on day one | Maximum leverage means thin cash flow and less room for vacancies and repairs |
| Rental income can support the debt over time | You become a landlord, or pay one |
| Forced appreciation does not depend on market timing as much as a flip's sale does | Capital is tied up longer if the refinance is delayed or comes in low |

## Assumptions

- The appraisal matches your ARV.
- The lender lends at the LTV you entered and approves the deal at your rent.
- Rent and operating costs are stable. The operating percentage covers everything except principal and interest.
- 30-year fully amortizing loan at a fixed rate.
- No seasoning delay beyond your plan.

## Limitations

- **No DSCR output.** Calculate it by hand with your lender's definition.
- **All-in cost is purchase plus rehab** unless you add other costs to the rehab field.
- **No seasoning, appraisal or lender approval model.** These decide whether the refinance happens at all.
- **No appreciation, rent growth, principal paydown or tax effects.**
- **Not a loan offer.** Rates, LTVs and DSCR minimums come from lenders.

## Common mistakes

1. **Leaving holding, financing and closing costs out of all-in cost.** Cash left looks smaller than it is.
2. **Assuming the maximum LTV.** Plan on a lower one and treat anything better as upside.
3. **Using lender DSCR as your cash flow test.** It usually leaves out vacancy, management, maintenance and reserves.
4. **Not confirming seasoning before buying.** A refinance you cannot do for another six months means six more months of rehab-loan interest.
5. **Using asking rents.** Use leased comps or a property manager's opinion.
6. **Doing a flip-grade rehab on a rental.** Tenants are hard on finishes. Durable materials save money over time.
7. **Treating "infinite return" as a goal.** It usually means maximum debt and minimum cash flow.

## BRRRR glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ARV | After repair value: the appraised value of the finished property |
| All-in cost | Everything you spent to get the property rented: purchase, rehab, closing, holding, financing |
| Cash-out refinance | A new loan larger than the existing one, with the difference paid to you |
| Capital recovery | Cash returned at refinance divided by the cash you put in |
| Cash left in the deal | All-in cost minus the new loan |
| Cash-on-cash return | Annual cash flow divided by cash left in the deal |
| DSCR | Debt service coverage ratio: income divided by the loan payment, as your lender defines it |
| DSCR loan | A rental loan underwritten mainly on the property's rent rather than the borrower's income |
| LTV | Loan-to-value: the loan as a share of appraised value |
| PITIA | Principal, interest, taxes, insurance and association dues |
| Seasoning | How long you must own the property, or how old the existing loan must be, before a lender will refinance on the new value |
| Delayed financing | A Fannie Mae exception that can let a cash buyer refinance soon after purchase, subject to its conditions |

## FAQ

### What does BRRRR stand for?

Buy, rehab, rent, refinance, repeat. You buy a property that needs work, renovate it, rent it, refinance on the higher value, and reuse the returned cash.

### What is a good DSCR for a BRRRR refinance?

Around 1.20–1.25 is a common rule of thumb in rental-loan underwriting, meaning rent covers the full payment with 20–25% to spare. Lenders set their own minimums and definitions, and some price loans differently below certain ratios. Ask yours.

### What is capital recovery?

The share of the cash you invested that the refinance returned. If you put in $27,300 and the refinance returned $23,000, your capital recovery is about 84%.

### What is an infinite return in BRRRR?

When the refinance returns all of your cash, cash left in the deal is zero or negative, and cash-on-cash return is mathematically infinite. It is a sign of maximum leverage, not necessarily a great rental. Judge the cash flow.

### What LTV should I use for the refinance?

Use the LTV your lender actually quotes for a cash-out refinance on a rental. Then run a lower one. A plan that only works at the highest LTV has no margin.

### How long do I have to wait before refinancing?

It depends on the lender and loan type. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, for example, generally requires at least one borrower on title for six months before a cash-out refinance and requires an existing first mortgage being paid off to be at least 12 months old, with some exceptions such as delayed financing for cash purchases. Other lenders, including DSCR lenders, set their own rules. Confirm before you buy.

### Is cash from a cash-out refinance taxable?

Refinance proceeds are borrowed money you must repay, so they are generally not treated as income. How the interest is treated, and how the extra debt affects you when you eventually sell, depend on your facts. This is a question for a CPA.

### What happens if the appraisal comes in low?

The new loan shrinks, so more of your cash stays in the deal. Your cash flow usually improves because the loan is smaller. Plan for an appraisal below your ARV.

### Does the 70% rule apply to BRRRR?

Some investors use a similar percentage on purchases, often tied to the refinance LTV. The better test is the full calculation: all-in cost vs the new loan, and cash flow after all expenses. See [how to analyze a BRRRR deal](/guides/how-to-analyze-a-brrrr-deal).

### Should I BRRRR or flip this house?

Run it both ways. A house with strong rent relative to its value is a BRRRR candidate. A house in an area where values are high relative to rent is usually a better flip. See [BRRRR vs flip](/guides/brrrr-vs-flip-which-fits-your-capital).
