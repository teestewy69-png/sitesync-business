The 70% rule says a flipper should pay no more than 70% of after repair value, minus repairs. It is popular because it is fast and it fits on a napkin. It is dangerous for the same reason: a single percentage stands in for interest, points, taxes, insurance, closing costs on both ends, time and profit, none of which it can see.

Used as a first screen, it saves you from wasting time on overpriced houses. Used as a final answer, it can talk you into a deal that loses money. This guide shows where the line is.

## Key takeaways

- **The rule is a screen.** It tells you which houses deserve full underwriting, not which ones to buy.
- **It works best in the middle:** mid-priced houses, cosmetic-to-mid rehabs, normal holds and normal financing.
- **It breaks at the edges:** cheap houses, expensive houses, heavy rehabs, long holds, expensive money and uncertain ARVs.
- **FlipRadar's market rule: tighten the percentage in a hot market, and loosen it in a slower market only if the full numbers still protect your margin.**
- **Do not subtract holding costs from the 70% MAO again.** The 30% is already supposed to cover them. If it does not, your percentage is wrong.
- **The best replacement is your own percentage,** built from your costs and profit target, and a full Maximum Purchase Price check on anything you put under contract.

## How to apply the 70% rule

1. **Start with a conservative ARV** from adjusted, sold comps.
2. **Use a complete rehab budget** with contingency.
3. **Calculate MAO = ARV × 70% − rehab** in the [70% rule calculator](/tools/70-percent-rule-calculator).
4. **Check the edges** in the "when it breaks" list below. If any apply, adjust the percentage or go straight to a full calculation.
5. **Adjust for the market** using FlipRadar's rule.
6. **Confirm with full numbers** using the [Maximum Purchase Price guide](/guides/maximum-purchase-price) before you sign.

## Inputs explained

| Input | Why it matters to the rule |
|---|---|
| ARV | The rule multiplies it. Every dollar of ARV error becomes 70 cents of offer error |
| Rehab | Subtracted dollar for dollar. Every overrun comes straight off your margin |
| Percentage | Stands in for every other cost and your profit. It is only right if those costs are typical for you |

## When the 70% rule works

The rule is a reasonable first screen when:

- The house is in the middle of its market's price range.
- The scope is cosmetic to mid-level, with systems inspected.
- The hold will be close to typical for your market.
- Financing costs are typical for you (or the deal is all cash).
- Your comps agree, so the ARV is not a guess.
- You are screening many leads quickly and will fully underwrite the few that pass.

## When the 70% rule breaks

### 1. Cheap houses

Many costs do not scale with price: title, insurance, lender fees, a minimum contractor charge, months of utilities. On a hypothetical $120,000-ARV house with a $35,000 rehab, the 70% MAO is $49,000. After typical costs, the profit might be roughly $15,000: about 12% of ARV, but very few dollars for months of work and the same risk as a bigger deal. Tighten the percentage or set a dollar floor.

### 2. Expensive houses

On a high-ARV house, 30% can be a large dollar cushion. Sticking to 70% may mean never winning a deal even when the full numbers show plenty of profit. Run the full calculation instead of loosening the percentage on instinct.

### 3. Heavy rehabs

Big scopes carry more variance. The rule subtracts your estimate, not the real number. The bigger the rehab, the more room you need for the gap between the two.

### 4. Long holds and expensive money

The 30% does not know whether you hold for four months or ten, or whether you pay 7% or 12% with three points. Long permits, slow markets and expensive financing all eat into the buffer.

### 5. Uncertain ARV

If your comps disagree, the rule turns a guess into a precise-looking offer. Fix the ARV first.

### 6. Hot markets

Competition pushes buyers to stretch, and comps from a fast-rising market may already be stretched. If the market cools during your hold, you take the hit at the sale. That is why FlipRadar tightens the percentage in hot markets.

## Hot vs slow markets: FlipRadar's rule

**In a hot market, tighten the percentage** (a lower % of ARV, which lowers your max offer). The reasoning:

- It is easier to overpay when buyers are competing.
- Comps may already reflect prices that are stretched relative to what buyers will pay at your sale date.
- Your sale is months away. A cooling market during your hold lands directly on your profit.
- You need more cushion when the downside is larger, not less.

**In a slower market, you can loosen the percentage only if the deal and the exit still protect your margin.** The reasoning:

- Less competition can mean more motivated sellers, better terms and comps that are less likely to be inflated by momentum.
- But slower markets usually mean longer days on market, so your hold and holding costs grow.
- Loosen only after you have priced the longer hold and confirmed your profit with the full calculation.

**Example ranges** some investors use are roughly 60–65% in a heated market and up to 75% in a slower one. These are examples, not a law. Your percentage should come from your own costs.

Much of the advice you will find says the opposite: raise the percentage in a hot market so you can win deals. FlipRadar disagrees. Raising your percentage in a hot market means paying more, with less cushion, at the moment values are most likely to be stretched. Winning a deal at the wrong price is not winning.

## Formula

```
70% MAO                 = ARV × 0.70 − Rehab
Your own percentage     = 1 − (Selling % + Buying % + Holding and financing % + Target profit %)
                          (each as a share of ARV)
Your MAO                = ARV × Your percentage − Rehab
```

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar's sample property: ARV $295,000, rehab $70,000, asking price $185,000. All numbers are hypothetical.

**Step 1: the 70% screen.** $295,000 × 0.70 − $70,000 = **$136,500.** The asking price is $48,500 above the MAO. The house fails the screen at the asking price.

**Step 2: what the 30% has to cover at $136,500.** With hypothetical costs (1.5% buying costs, a hard money loan at 11% with 2 points and $2,750 in fees, $700 a month in carrying costs for six months, 7% selling costs):

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| 30% buffer | $88,500 |
| Buying costs | about −$2,050 |
| Financing | about −$15,100 |
| Holding | −$4,200 |
| Selling | −$20,650 |
| **Profit before tax** | **about $46,500** |

**Step 3: do not subtract holding twice.** A common mistake is to take the $136,500 MAO and subtract holding costs again, giving a lower "adjusted MAO." The holding costs are already inside the 30% buffer, as the table shows. Subtracting them again either double counts them or tells you your percentage is wrong. If your costs are higher than the rule assumes, lower the percentage once.

**Step 4: build your own percentage.** For this deal type, selling costs are about 7.0% of ARV, buying costs about 0.7%, holding and financing about 6.7%. With a 12% profit target: 1 − 26.4% ≈ **73.6%**, which gives an MAO of about $147,100. The full Maximum Purchase Price calculation lands in the same place (about $147,000), because both are built from the same costs.

**Step 5: apply the market rule.** In a hot market with fast-rising comps, a cautious investor might lower that to the high 60s and offer accordingly. In a slower market with longer days on market, the investor might keep the 73–74% only after re-running the costs with a longer hold.

**Step 6: what happens at the asking price.** Buying at $185,000 with the same costs produces a loss of about $5,800 before tax. The seller's price is not a negotiating anchor. Your numbers are.

## What the result means

- **Asking price below your MAO:** the deal is worth full underwriting.
- **Asking price above your MAO:** negotiate with your numbers, change the plan, or pass. Do not move the percentage to make it fit.
- **MAO close to asking:** treat as a fail unless the full calculation shows your profit target after a longer hold and a lower sale price.
- **Negative MAO:** the rehab is larger than the gross ceiling. No price makes it work as a flip at this scope.

## Assumptions

- The ARV and rehab are honest, conservative estimates.
- Your costs are close to the costs the percentage implies.
- Your hold and financing are typical for you.

## Limitations

- One number cannot show time, interest, points or closing costs separately.
- Accuracy falls off at low and high price points.
- It does not apply to rentals, which depend on rent, expenses and refinance terms.
- It is no substitute for a lender's appraisal and approval.

## Common mistakes

1. **Calling the 30% profit.**
2. **Subtracting holding or closing costs a second time.**
3. **Raising the percentage to win a bidding war.**
4. **Using the rule on a rental.**
5. **Keeping the same percentage when your financing, insurance or selling costs change.**
6. **Trusting the rule on a cheap house** where the dollar profit is too small for the risk.
7. **Using a hopeful ARV.** The rule makes a bad ARV look precise.

## FAQ

### What is the 70% rule?

A rule of thumb that says to pay no more than 70% of a property's after repair value, minus repairs, when buying a flip. The remaining 30% is meant to cover all other costs and your profit.

### Should I use a lower or higher percentage in a hot market?

Lower. FlipRadar's rule is to tighten the percentage in a hot market because overpaying is easier, comps may be stretched, and a cooling market during your hold lands on your sale price. Example ranges some investors use are around 60–65%, but your number should come from your own costs.

### Can I use a higher percentage in a slower market?

Sometimes, if the full numbers still protect your margin after a longer hold. Less competition can help on price and terms, but longer days on market add holding costs. Loosen only after you have re-run the full calculation.

### Is the 30% all profit?

No. It has to cover buying, holding, financing and selling costs before any profit. On FlipRadar's sample deal, those costs take about 14% of ARV.

### Does the 70% rule include closing costs?

Yes, implicitly. Closing costs on both the purchase and the sale are meant to come out of the 30%. Do not subtract them again from the MAO.

### Why does the 70% rule fail on cheap houses?

Because many costs are fixed in dollars. A small house can pass the rule and still produce too little profit for the work and risk. Set a dollar floor as well as a percentage.

### Is the 70% rule still useful with higher interest rates?

Yes as a screen, but higher rates, more points and longer holds consume more of the 30%. Recalculate your own percentage when your financing changes.

### What should I use instead of the 70% rule?

Your own percentage for screening, and a full Maximum Purchase Price calculation before you sign. See the [Maximum Purchase Price guide](/guides/maximum-purchase-price).

### Do wholesalers use the 70% rule?

Many do. A wholesaler takes the end buyer's MAO and subtracts the assignment fee to find the most they can agree to pay the seller.
