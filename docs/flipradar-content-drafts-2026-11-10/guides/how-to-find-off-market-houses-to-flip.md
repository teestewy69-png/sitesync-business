"Off-market" sounds like a secret discount. It is not. It is a different trade: you spend money and time finding sellers before they list, in exchange for less competition and more room to solve a seller's problem. Some off-market deals are great. Many are priced at retail by owners who simply did not call an agent. And some of the best flips start on the MLS, in listings other investors scrolled past.

This guide compares the main sources, what each one costs you, and how to tell whether a source is actually working.

## Key takeaways

- **The discount comes from the seller's situation, not from the source.** A motivated seller on the MLS beats an unmotivated one in your mailbox.
- **On-market deals are underrated.** Stale listings, poor photos, as-is listings, estates and price cuts are all public, and many investors ignore them.
- **Off-market is a marketing business.** Measure cost per signed deal, not cost per lead.
- **Auctions and sheriff sales trade inspection and title certainty for price.** Know the local rules before you bid.
- **Wholesalers save you marketing cost and charge for it.** Underwrite their deals from scratch; their ARV and repair numbers are sales copy.
- **Run every lead through the same math:** ARV, rehab, MAO. The source does not change the numbers.

## How to apply this

1. **Pick a farm area** you know well enough to price quickly.
2. **Start with low-cost sources:** the MLS through an investor-friendly agent, expired listings, FSBOs, driving for dollars, public records and your local investor network.
3. **Add paid sources one at a time** (mail, purchased lists, online ads), with a fixed budget and a tracking sheet.
4. **Track every lead** from source to signed contract to closed deal.
5. **Calculate cost per deal** for each source after a fair test period, and keep only what works.
6. **Screen every lead the same way** with the [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator) and [70% rule calculator](/tools/70-percent-rule-calculator) before you make contact or an offer.

## Inputs explained: the sources

### On-market vs off-market

| | On-market (MLS) | Off-market |
|---|---|---|
| Competition | Visible to every buyer | Fewer buyers, sometimes just you |
| Price discovery | Seller usually has an agent's pricing | Seller may not know value, in either direction |
| Your cost | Agent relationship, speed, offers | Marketing spend, time, follow-up |
| Information | Listing data, disclosures, photos | Whatever you can learn from the seller and records |
| Best opportunities | Stale listings, price cuts, as-is, estates, poor marketing | Inherited, vacant, tired landlords, deferred maintenance, pre-foreclosure |
| Main risk | Bidding up to retail | Spending more on marketing than the discount is worth |

### On-market sources

- **Investor-friendly agents.** An agent who knows what you buy and sends you listings that fit, writes fast offers, and tells you about pocket listings and upcoming estates. Pay them well and close what you say you will close.
- **Stale and as-is listings.** High days on market, multiple price cuts, "cash only," "as-is," bad photos, or remarks like "needs TLC."
- **Expired and withdrawn listings.** Sellers who tried and failed to sell can be more flexible. Some agents and data services track them.
- **Bank-owned (REO) listings.** Banks list foreclosures on the MLS. Process can be slow and rigid, but pricing is often realistic.

### Off-market sources

- **Driving for dollars.** Spot neglected houses (overgrown yards, boarded windows, piled mail), look up the owner in county records, and reach out. Cheap in money, expensive in time.
- **Direct mail.** Letters or postcards to targeted lists: absentee owners, inherited properties, long-term owners with high equity, code violations, tax delinquencies. Success depends on list quality and repeated mailings.
- **Buying leads and lists.** Data providers sell owner lists with filters. Lead-generation companies sell inbound seller leads, often to more than one investor. Ask whether leads are exclusive.
- **Networking and REIAs.** Local real estate investor associations and meetups put you in a room with wholesalers, lenders, contractors and other flippers. Many deals trade between investors who know each other.
- **Wholesalers.** They find off-market sellers, put the house under contract, and assign the contract to you for a fee. You save the marketing cost and pay the fee. Verify everything yourself.
- **Probate and estates.** Heirs often want a fast, simple sale of a house that needs work. Probate filings are generally public; contact with grieving families needs care and patience.
- **Pre-foreclosure and tax delinquency.** Public notices identify owners under pressure. These sellers need solutions, not pressure. Rules on contacting and buying from homeowners in foreclosure vary by state; talk to a local attorney.
- **FSBO.** Owners selling themselves. Some are realistic, some are priced well above market.

### Auctions and sheriff sales

Foreclosure auctions, sheriff sales and tax sales can produce low prices, with real trade-offs:

- **Usually no interior inspection.** You bid on what you can see from the street and in the records.
- **Cash or near-cash terms**, often with a deposit at the sale and the balance within a short period.
- **Title risk.** Depending on the sale type and state, some liens may survive. Get a title search before you bid.
- **Occupancy.** The house may be occupied. Removing occupants follows state law and takes time and money.
- **Redemption rights.** Some states allow the former owner to redeem after the sale.

Read the county's sale rules, watch a few auctions before you bid, and price the unknowns into a lower percentage.

### Online lead generation

A website and online ads can bring inbound sellers. It works when you can answer fast, follow up for months, and track cost per deal. Paid search and social ads can get expensive in competitive markets. Organic search takes time. If you call or text sellers, learn the federal and state rules on telemarketing and texting first.

## Formula

```
Cost per lead      = Spend ÷ Leads
Cost per contract  = Spend ÷ Signed contracts
Cost per deal      = Spend ÷ Closed deals
Source is working  = (Profit per deal from that source − Cost per deal) is positive and better than alternatives
```

Track spend, leads, appointments, offers, contracts and closings by source. Lead counts are vanity. Closed deals are the only number that pays.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

These figures are hypothetical and are not response-rate benchmarks. They show how to measure a source, not what to expect from one.

An investor tests three sources for six months in one farm area:

| Source | Spend | Leads | Contracts | Closed deals | Cost per deal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct mail to inherited and absentee owners | $9,000 | 60 | 2 | 1 | $9,000 |
| Investor-friendly agent (stale and as-is listings) | $0 upfront (commission paid at closing by seller or as agreed) | 25 offers made | 3 | 2 | Time and relationship |
| Wholesaler deals | $12,000 in assignment fees | 15 deals reviewed | 1 | 1 | $12,000 (paid at closing) |

**Reading it:** the mail campaign produced one deal for $9,000 of marketing. That is fine if the deal's profit justified it, and a problem if it was a thin deal. The agent produced two deals with no marketing spend, but the investor made 25 offers to get three accepted. The wholesaler deal cost $12,000, but only at closing and only on a deal that passed the investor's own underwriting.

Every lead, whatever the source, went through the same screen: FlipRadar's sample house, for example, came in from an agent at an asking price of $185,000. With an ARV of $295,000 and a $70,000 rehab, the 70% MAO was $136,500 and the full Maximum Purchase Price about $147,000. The investor offered in that range, explained the numbers, and closed at $145,000 after the listing sat. The source did not make it a deal. The numbers did.

## What the result means

- **A source with a high cost per deal can still be worth it** if the deals it produces have bigger margins.
- **A free source is not free.** Agent relationships and driving for dollars cost time. Count it.
- **If a source produces leads but no contracts,** the problem is usually the list, the message or your follow-up, not the volume.
- **If you are winning many deals at your first offer,** your offers may be too high.

## Assumptions

- You track sources consistently from first contact to closing.
- Your underwriting does not change by source.
- You test long enough to see results; direct marketing often needs repeated touches.

## Limitations

- Results vary widely by market, season and competition. This guide does not predict response rates or costs.
- Rules on telemarketing, texting, wholesaling, foreclosure contact and auctions vary by state and change. Check current law and talk to a local attorney before you start a campaign or bid at a sale.
- Some sources (probate, pre-foreclosure) involve people under stress. Treat sellers fairly and honestly.

## Common mistakes

1. **Assuming off-market means discounted.**
2. **Ignoring the MLS** because "everyone else is on it."
3. **Trusting a wholesaler's ARV and repair numbers.**
4. **Bidding at auction without a title search** or a plan for occupancy.
5. **Measuring leads instead of closed deals.**
6. **Mailing once and quitting.** Or mailing forever without measuring.
7. **Raising offers to "win" an off-market seller** who is not motivated.
8. **Pressuring sellers in hardship.** It is wrong, and in some states it is also illegal.

## FAQ

### What is an off-market property?

A property sold without being listed on the MLS. The seller may sell directly to an investor, through a wholesaler, at auction, or through a private network.

### Are off-market deals cheaper than MLS deals?

Not automatically. The discount comes from a motivated seller and a problem you can solve. Many off-market sellers expect full price, and many MLS listings sell at a discount because they are stale or need work.

### What is the best way to find houses to flip?

The best source is the one with the lowest cost per closed deal in your market, given your time and money. Most investors combine an investor-friendly agent, their local network, and one or two paid marketing channels they track closely.

### How do I find off-market houses for free?

Driving for dollars, public records, expired listings, FSBOs and local investor networking cost time rather than money.

### Should I buy leads?

Test them with a fixed budget. Ask whether leads are exclusive or shared with other investors, and measure cost per closed deal, not cost per lead.

### Are foreclosure auctions and sheriff sales good for flips?

They can be, if you understand the local rules. You usually cannot inspect, terms are cash or near cash, some liens may survive, and the property may be occupied. Price those risks into your offer.

### How do I work with wholesalers?

Build relationships with a few reliable ones, tell them exactly what you buy, and underwrite every deal yourself. Expect to pay their fee at closing and confirm the contract is assignable.

### Do I need a real estate license to find deals?

Not to buy for yourself. Some states regulate wholesaling or marketing contracts you do not own, so check local rules if you plan to assign contracts.

### What is a REIA?

A real estate investor association: a local group where investors, wholesalers, lenders and contractors meet. It is one of the cheapest ways to build a deal network.
