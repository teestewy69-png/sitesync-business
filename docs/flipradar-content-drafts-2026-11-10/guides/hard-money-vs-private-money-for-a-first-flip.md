Both hard money and private money fund flips that banks will not. The difference is who is on the other side. A hard money lender is a business with a process, a term sheet and a lawyer-drafted set of loan documents. A private lender is a person, often someone who trusts you, lending without the process unless you build it.

For a first flip, that difference matters more than the rate. This guide covers the mechanics a first-time borrower usually learns the hard way: what is in a hard money term sheet, how to structure private money so it protects both sides, and what each one costs on the same deal.

## Key takeaways

- **Hard money buys you speed, a process and a lender who has seen many flips.** You pay in rate, points, fees and rules.
- **Private money can be cheaper and more flexible.** You pay in relationship risk, and you have to supply the structure.
- **Read the term sheet for the clauses, not the rate:** interest on drawn vs full balance, minimum interest, extension terms, default rate, personal guarantee.
- **Private money needs the same paperwork a lender would use:** a promissory note, a recorded mortgage or deed of trust, title insurance, and insurance naming the lender.
- **Borrowing from several passive investors can raise securities law issues.** Talk to an attorney before you take money from people who are not making their own lending decisions.
- **First flip rule of thumb:** borrow from someone who will still be fine if the flip goes badly.

## How to apply this

1. **Underwrite the deal first** with the [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator), the [rehab cost estimator](/tools/rehab-cost-estimator) and the [Maximum Purchase Price guide](/guides/maximum-purchase-price). Lenders of both kinds want to see it.
2. **Get two or three hard money term sheets** to learn current terms, even if you plan to use private money. They give you a benchmark.
3. **If using private money,** write a one-page term summary first: amount, rate, points, payment schedule, maturity, security, what happens if you are late or over budget.
4. **Compare total cost and cash needed** on your realistic hold and a long hold.
5. **Pick the option whose worst case you can live with,** not the one with the best best case.

## Inputs explained

### What is in a hard money term sheet

| Term | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Loan amount and caps | What share of purchase and rehab? What LTC and LTARV limits? Who values the ARV? |
| Rate | Fixed? Interest-only? Paid monthly or deferred? |
| Interest method | On the full commitment, or only on funds drawn? |
| Points | On the full commitment or the initial advance? Any exit points? |
| Fees | Underwriting, processing, documents, valuation, draw inspections, wires, payoff |
| Draws | Schedule, inspection method, typical turnaround, retainage |
| Term and extensions | Length, extension availability, extension fee and rate |
| Minimum interest and prepayment | Any minimum months of interest if you sell early? |
| Default terms | Default interest rate, late fees, what triggers default |
| Guaranty and recourse | Personal guarantee required? Full recourse? |
| Borrower requirements | Entity, credit, reserves, experience tier |

How lenders treat first-time borrowers varies, but common patterns are lower leverage, higher pricing, or a requirement for more cash reserves. Some lenders offer better terms if an experienced partner joins the deal. The [hard money loan calculator](/tools/hard-money-loan-calculator) page walks through draws, LTC vs LTARV, underwriting documents and extensions in detail.

### How private money is usually structured

| Element | What it does |
|---|---|
| Promissory note | The borrower's promise to repay: amount, rate, payment terms, maturity, default terms |
| Mortgage or deed of trust, recorded | Secures the loan against the property. Recording puts the lender in a lien position |
| Lien position | A first-position lender is paid first from a sale or foreclosure. Know where your lender stands |
| Lender's title insurance policy | Protects the lender against title defects |
| Insurance naming the lender | Builder's risk or vacant property coverage with the lender as mortgagee or loss payee |
| Draw or disbursement terms | If the lender funds rehab, how and when |
| Personal guarantee (optional) | Some private lenders ask for one |
| Closing through a title or escrow company or attorney | Puts the money through a neutral party and records documents properly |

### Debt vs equity

A private **lender** earns interest and gets repaid first. A private **partner** owns part of the deal and shares profit and loss. Mixing the two ("10% interest plus a share of profit") is common, but write down which one it is and what happens if the deal loses money. Equity partners usually get paid last.

### A securities law caution

Raising money from people who are passive (they rely on you to make the investment decisions) can, in some situations, involve federal or state securities laws, especially when you take money from several people or advertise. A one-to-one loan secured by a specific property is a different situation from pooling money from friends. This is a question for a securities attorney before you raise anything.

## Formula

```
Total financing cost = Points + Fees + Interest (+ extension fees, default costs if they occur)
Cash needed at start = Down payment + Buying closing costs + Points and upfront fees
                     + First rehab phase before reimbursement + Reserves
Monthly cash need    = Interest payments (if paid monthly) + Carrying costs
```

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar's sample deal: purchase $145,000, rehab $70,000, ARV $295,000, six-month hold. Both loans fund 85% of purchase ($123,250) and 100% of rehab ($70,000) in four $17,500 draws: a $193,250 commitment. All terms are hypothetical.

| | Hard money | Private money |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | 11%, paid monthly | 10%, accrued and paid at sale |
| Interest method | On funds drawn | On funds drawn |
| Points | 2 ($3,865) | 1 ($1,933) |
| Fees | $2,750 (underwriting, docs, four draw inspections) | $1,000 (attorney-prepared note and mortgage) |
| Interest over six months | about $9,025 | about $8,204 |
| **Total financing cost** | **about $15,640** | **about $11,137** |
| Upfront cash (down payment + closing costs + points and fees) | about $29,540 | about $26,860 |
| Monthly interest out of pocket | yes, rising with draws | none until sale |
| Draw process | Lender inspection, fixed schedule | As agreed; you must build the controls |
| If the flip runs 3 months long | Extension fee likely, at lender's discretion | Renegotiate with a person you know |

**Reading it:** the private loan saves about $4,500 and is easier on monthly cash flow. The hard money loan comes with a process: a lender who checks the scope, inspects draws, and has a clear rule for extensions. On a first flip, that process is part of what you are buying. If you use private money, put the same discipline in writing: a scope, a draw schedule tied to completed work, and an agreed plan if the project runs late or over budget.

**The question that decides it:** picture this flip losing $20,000 and taking four extra months, and ask which lender relationship survives. If the honest answer is neither, the problem is the deal or the leverage, not the lender.

## What the result means

- **Hard money is usually the better first choice when** you need to close fast, you do not have a private lender who can absorb a loss, or you want an outside check on your scope and budget.
- **Private money is usually the better choice when** the lender is experienced, understands the risk, can afford a delay or a loss, and agrees to proper documentation.
- **Using both** is common: hard money for the senior loan, private money for part of the down payment. Make sure the hard money lender allows a second lien or other financing, and that your private lender understands they are behind the first lien.

### Pros and cons

| | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Hard money | Fast, repeatable process; lends on the deal; draws and inspections add discipline; clear terms | Higher rate, points and fees; strict rules on extensions and defaults; leverage limited for new borrowers |
| Private money | Flexible terms; can be cheaper; can defer interest; relationship-based | Relationship risk; you must supply structure and paperwork; possible securities law issues when raising from passive investors |

## Assumptions

- Both lenders lend on the same caps and draw schedule for comparison.
- The deal closes on time and sells at the ARV.
- Private-money paperwork is prepared by an attorney and recorded.

## Limitations

- Terms vary widely by lender, market and borrower. Use current term sheets.
- This guide does not cover bank, credit union, home equity or seller financing.
- Not legal advice. Loan documents, guarantees, usury limits and securities rules depend on your state and your facts.

## Common mistakes

1. **Choosing on rate alone.**
2. **Taking private money on a handshake** with no note, no recorded lien and no title policy.
3. **Borrowing from someone who cannot afford to lose the money.**
4. **Not reading the guaranty.**
5. **Skipping the draw controls on a private loan.**
6. **Stacking a private second lien without the first lender's permission.**
7. **Promising returns you cannot guarantee.** Describe the risk honestly.
8. **Raising money from several passive investors without legal advice.**

## FAQ

### What is the difference between hard money and private money?

Hard money comes from businesses that make short-term real estate loans as their main line of work, with standard terms and processes. Private money comes from individuals, usually people you know, with terms you negotiate.

### Which is cheaper for a first flip?

Private money is often cheaper in rate and fees, but not always. Compare total cost, cash needed and what happens if the project runs long.

### Do hard money lenders lend to first-time flippers?

Many do, often with lower leverage, higher pricing or larger reserve requirements than for experienced borrowers. Some offer better terms with an experienced partner on the deal.

### How do I protect a private lender?

Use a promissory note, a recorded mortgage or deed of trust, a lender's title policy and insurance naming the lender, and close through a title company, escrow company or attorney.

### What is lien position?

The order in which lenders get paid from a sale or foreclosure. A first-position lender is paid before a second. Private lenders should know exactly where they stand.

### Can I use private money for my down payment and hard money for the rest?

Often, yes, but the hard money lender must allow it, and the private lender should understand they are in a riskier position behind the first loan.

### Should private money interest be paid monthly or at sale?

Either can work. Deferring interest to the sale eases monthly cash flow but increases what is owed at payoff. Write it into the note.

### Is borrowing from friends and family risky?

Yes, for the relationship as much as the money. Borrow only from people who understand the risk and could absorb a delay or a loss.

### Do I need an attorney for private money?

It is strongly advisable. An attorney can draft the note and security documents, check state lending rules, and advise on whether securities laws apply.
