**This is not tax advice.** It is a plain-English map of the questions that decide how flip profit is taxed, with links to the IRS pages behind them. Tax treatment depends on your facts. Confirm yours with a qualified tax professional before you plan around any number.

Many flipping articles, including an earlier version of this page, describe flip profit as a "short-term capital gain." Others say it is "ordinary income plus self-employment tax." Both can be true for different people. The deciding question is not just how long you owned the house. It is **why** you owned it.

## Key takeaways

- **Holding period decides short-term vs long-term, but only for capital assets.** The IRS says assets held one year or less produce short-term gains, and net short-term capital gains are taxed at ordinary income rates.
- **A house held mainly for sale to customers in a trade or business is not a capital asset,** according to IRS Publication 544. For someone treated as a real estate dealer, flip profit is generally ordinary business income, not a capital gain of any kind.
- **Self-employment tax may apply to dealer income.** The IRS lists the self-employment tax rate as 15.3% (12.4% Social Security and 2.9% Medicare), with limits and adjustments figured on Schedule SE.
- **Investor vs dealer depends on facts,** such as how often you flip, how you hold and market properties, and how you describe the activity. There is no single bright-line test on this page, and you should not rely on one from any article.
- **Entity, state law and how the deal is structured matter too.** So does whether you lived in the house.
- **Plan for the tax before you reinvest the proceeds,** and ask about estimated payments.

## How to apply this

1. **Before you buy,** decide how you will hold the property and keep records consistent with that: purchase documents, entity, bank accounts, marketing.
2. **Track every cost by category:** purchase and closing costs, rehab, holding costs, financing costs, selling costs. Keep receipts.
3. **Estimate the tax two ways** (as ordinary business income with self-employment tax, and as a short-term capital gain) so you see the range before your CPA tells you which applies.
4. **Set aside cash** for the higher estimate until your CPA confirms.
5. **Ask about estimated tax payments** for the quarter of the sale.
6. **Bring the questions at the end of this page** to a CPA or enrolled agent who works with real estate investors.

### Questions to bring to your CPA

- Based on my activity, am I likely to be treated as a dealer or an investor on this property?
- If dealer, how will self-employment tax apply, and what can I do about it legally?
- How will my rehab, holding, financing and selling costs be treated, and when?
- Should I make an estimated payment in the quarter of the sale?
- Does my entity structure make sense for flips at my volume?
- How does my state tax this income?

## Inputs explained: the questions that decide the treatment

| Question | Why it matters |

### Dealer vs investor

The IRS publications cited here define what is **not** a capital asset (property held mainly for sale to customers in a trade or business) but do not give flippers a simple checklist for when they cross that line. Factors commonly discussed by tax professionals include how many properties you buy and sell, how quickly, how much work you do to them, how you market them, and how much of your time and income the activity represents. Someone who buys, renovates and resells houses regularly as a business is more likely to be treated as a dealer than someone who sells a single investment property.

Treat this as a conversation for your CPA, not a box to tick yourself.

### What changes if you are treated as a dealer

- **Ordinary income.** Profit is generally ordinary business income rather than capital gain.
- **Self-employment tax may apply.** The Schedule SE instructions say gains from capital assets, and from property that is not inventory or held primarily for sale to customers, are not included in net earnings from self-employment. Dealer property is the exception, so dealer profit may be subject to self-employment tax. The IRS lists the rate as 15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security (on combined earnings up to $184,500 for 2026) and 2.9% for Medicare, with an additional 0.9% Medicare tax above certain income thresholds. The tax is figured on Schedule SE, and the employer-equivalent portion is deductible in figuring adjusted gross income.
- **No like-kind exchange.** The Form 8824 instructions state that section 1031 does not apply to real property held primarily for sale.
- **Timing of costs.** How and when your costs are recovered can differ for property treated as inventory. Ask your CPA how your rehab, holding and financing costs will be handled.

### What changes if you are treated as an investor

- **Capital gain or loss.** For property held one year or less, a net short-term capital gain is taxed at ordinary income rates, per IRS Topic 409. Long-term rates may apply after more than one year.
- **Generally not self-employment income,** per the Schedule SE instructions' treatment of capital asset gains.
- **Capital loss limits** apply if the flip loses money (Topic 409 describes the $3,000 annual limit against ordinary income, $1,500 if married filing separately, with carryovers).

## Formula

These are the building blocks, not a tax calculation:

```
Gain or profit         = Amount realized on sale − Adjusted basis (or cost of the property sold)
Amount realized        = Sale price − Selling costs
Adjusted basis (simplified) = Purchase price + Purchase costs that are capitalized + Improvements
Self-employment tax    = Figured on Schedule SE on net earnings from self-employment, if applicable
```

Which costs go into basis, which are deductible as expenses, and when, depends on your facts and treatment. Your CPA will sort that out; your job is to give them clean records.

The gap between the two columns can be thousands of dollars on a single flip, before any state tax. That is why FlipRadar no longer describes flip profit simply as "short-term gains." The right label depends on your facts.

**The planning move:** set aside cash for the higher-tax framing until your CPA confirms which applies.

## What the result means

- **If you flip regularly,** expect your CPA to ask dealer questions, and expect that self-employment tax may be part of the answer.
- **If you sell one investment property,** capital gain treatment may apply. The holding period then decides short-term or long-term.
- **If you are near the one-year mark on a property held as an investment,** ask your CPA whether timing matters for you before you set a closing date.
- **If you use an entity,** ask how the entity is taxed and whether a different structure would change self-employment tax for your situation. Some flippers discuss S corporation structures with their CPA; whether that helps depends on your income, costs and state.

## Common mistakes

1. **Assuming every flip held under a year is a short-term capital gain.** It may be ordinary business income.
2. **Forgetting self-employment tax** in your profit planning.
3. **Reinvesting all proceeds** before setting aside tax.
4. **Mixing personal and project money,** which makes costs hard to prove.
5. **Losing receipts** for rehab, holding and closing costs.
6. **Planning a 1031 exchange on a flip.** It does not apply to property held primarily for sale.
7. **Skipping estimated payments** in the quarter of a profitable sale.
8. **Choosing an entity because a forum said so.** Ask your CPA with your numbers.

## FAQ

### Is flip profit a short-term capital gain or ordinary income?

It depends on your facts. If the house is a capital asset you held one year or less, the gain is short-term and taxed at ordinary income rates. If you are treated as a dealer holding property mainly for sale to customers, the house is not a capital asset and profit is generally ordinary business income. A tax professional can tell you which applies.

### Do flippers pay self-employment tax?

They may, if the profit is dealer or business income. The IRS lists the self-employment tax rate as 15.3%, figured on Schedule SE, with a Social Security wage base and an additional Medicare tax above certain thresholds. Gains on capital assets are generally not included in self-employment earnings.

### What makes someone a real estate dealer?

There is no simple checklist in the IRS publications cited here. Factors commonly considered include how often you buy and sell, how quickly, how much work you do, how you market properties and how central the activity is to your income. Ask a CPA.

### Can I use a 1031 exchange on a flip?

The IRS Form 8824 instructions state that section 1031 does not apply to real property held primarily for sale, which generally describes a flip.

### Does holding a flip more than a year make it a long-term gain?

Only if the property is a capital asset in your hands. For dealer property, holding longer does not convert business income into capital gain.

### Do I need to make estimated tax payments after a flip?

Possibly. The IRS says individuals generally must make estimated payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when they file. Ask your CPA about the quarter of your sale.

### Can an LLC or S corporation reduce taxes on flips?

It might change how income and self-employment tax are handled, or it might not, depending on your facts, costs and state. Ask a CPA to compare options with your numbers.

### What records should I keep for a flip?

Closing statements for the purchase and sale, every rehab invoice and receipt, loan documents and interest statements, insurance, utilities, property tax bills, and a log of your time and activities.

### Can I deduct my own labor on a flip?

You generally cannot deduct the value of your own time. Paid labor and materials are treated according to your tax status and the nature of the cost. Ask your CPA.

### Who should I talk to about flip taxes?

A CPA or enrolled agent who regularly works with real estate investors in your state.
