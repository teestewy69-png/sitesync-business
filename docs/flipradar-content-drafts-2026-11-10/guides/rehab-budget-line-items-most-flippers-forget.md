Most blown rehab budgets do not fail on the kitchen. The kitchen was priced. They fail on the lines nobody wrote down: the permit that triggered a panel upgrade, the sewer line that collapsed when the plumber ran water, the appliances that were "included" until they were not, the third dumpster.

This is a checklist of those lines. Use it during your walk-through and again before you sign a contract. The costs themselves are local. What matters here is that each line exists in your budget, with a number next to it.

## Key takeaways

- **The forgotten costs cluster in four places:** what is behind walls and underground, what the permit office requires, the small logistics of running a job site, and the work after the work (punch list, cleaning, staging).
- **Inspect what you cannot see before you buy:** sewer line, roof, electrical panel, foundation, and signs of water. These are cheap to check and expensive to discover.
- **Permits can create scope,** not just fees. Opening a wall or replacing a panel can trigger code upgrades elsewhere.
- **Houses built before 1978** may need lead-safe work practices from certified contractors under EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule. That affects who you hire and how they work.
- **Contingency covers unknowns, not forgotten knowns.** If it is on this list and applies to your house, budget it as scope.
- **Holding, financing, closing costs and taxes are not rehab.** They belong in your deal model. Keep them out of the rehab budget so you do not double count, and keep them in the profit model so you do not forget them.

## How to apply this checklist

1. **Walk the house with this list and your scope sheet.** For each item, mark: not applicable, included in a bid, or needs a number.
2. **Order the cheap inspections before your contingency period ends:** sewer camera, roof, electrical, and anything that suggests water or structural movement.
3. **Call the building department** with your scope. Ask what permits you need, current review and inspection times, and whether your scope triggers any required upgrades.
4. **Ask every contractor what is excluded** from their bid. Write exclusions into your budget as separate lines.
5. **Add the logistics lines** (utilities, dumpsters, toilet, lockbox, cleaning) even if they look small.
6. **Re-check the list at your first draw.** New findings move from contingency to scope.

## Inputs explained: the forgotten lines

### Behind walls and underground

| Line item | Why it gets missed | How to catch it before you buy |
|---|---|---|
| Sewer lateral (house to street) | Invisible until it backs up | Sewer camera inspection |
| Galvanized or failing supply pipes | Water still runs today | Check pipe material at the meter, water heater and under sinks |
| Electrical panel and wiring | Lights work, so it seems fine | Panel brand and amperage; look for old wiring types and double taps |
| Subfloor rot at toilets, tubs and laundry | Hidden under flooring | Soft spots, stains below in a basement or crawlspace |
| Roof decking | Shingles hide it | Roof inspection; check the attic for stains and daylight |
| Foundation and drainage | Cracks get painted over | Sticking doors, sloped floors, grading toward the house; engineer if in doubt |
| Hidden water damage and mold | Fresh paint and air freshener | Moisture meter, smell, staining around windows and in the attic |
| Termites and wood-destroying insects | Damage inside framing | Pest inspection |

### Permits and code

| Line item | Why it gets missed |
|---|---|
| Permit fees by trade | Budgeted as one small number instead of building, electrical, plumbing and mechanical |
| Permit-triggered upgrades | Opening walls or replacing a panel can require upgrades such as smoke and CO detectors, GFCI and AFCI protection, or egress windows, depending on local code |
| Plan review or engineering | Structural changes may need drawings or an engineer's letter |
| Re-inspection fees | Failed inspections cost money and days |
| Closing out old open permits | A previous owner's unfinished permit can stop your sale |
| Lead-safe work on pre-1978 homes | Paid contractors disturbing painted surfaces must be certified and follow lead-safe practices under EPA's RRP Rule. It can change bids and schedules |

### Running the job site

- Utility turn-on, deposits and the monthly bills during rehab
- Dumpsters and haul-off (often more than one)
- Portable toilet if the plumbing is offline
- Lockbox, locks rekeyed, and basic security
- Appliance purchase, delivery, installation and haul-away of old units
- Small tools, consumables, and the trip charges that add up when crews come back
- Builder's risk or vacant property insurance (often required by lenders, and it belongs in holding costs)

### The work after the work

- Punch list (touch-up paint, adjustments, missed trim)
- Final construction clean, and a second clean before photos
- Landscaping, mulch, and a first impression from the street
- Light fixtures, outlet covers, door stops, hardware, window coverings where expected
- Smoke and CO detectors
- Professional photos and staging
- Repairs requested after the buyer's inspection

### Soft costs that belong in the deal model, not the rehab

Holding costs, loan points and interest, buying and selling closing costs, and income taxes are real costs that first flips often forget. They are not construction costs, so keep them out of your rehab number and in your full deal model. See [holding costs on a 6-month flip](/guides/holding-costs-on-a-6-month-flip), the [hard money loan calculator](/tools/hard-money-loan-calculator) and the [flip taxes guide](/guides/flip-taxes-short-term-gains-basics-not-tax-advice).

## Formula

```
Rehab budget = Priced scope (all trades)
             + Permits, plan review and inspections
             + Site logistics (utilities during rehab, dumpsters, toilet, security)
             + Finish-out (punch list, cleaning, landscaping, staging)
             + Contingency % × (all of the above)
```

A quick completeness check: if your logistics and finish-out lines add up to almost nothing on a mid-level rehab, something is missing.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar's sample property: a 1,500 sq ft ranch built in 1968, with a $62,500 base scope and a 12% contingency in the [rehab cost estimator](/tools/rehab-cost-estimator) example. All numbers are hypothetical.

During the walk-through, the investor runs this checklist and finds:

| Finding | Action | Hypothetical budget effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1968 house with original painted trim and windows | Hire RRP-certified contractors for paint and trim work; confirm it in bids | Bids may rise; schedule may lengthen |
| Original clay sewer lateral | Sewer camera before the end of the inspection period | Inspection is inexpensive. If it shows root intrusion or a break, a repair can run thousands |
| 100-amp panel | Electrician confirms the panel upgrade will trigger AFCI and smoke detector requirements | Already in the $3,000 electrical line, confirmed by bid |
| Appliances "included" in the listing are 20 years old | Budget a new appliance package | Already in the $16,500 kitchen line |
| Builder's risk insurance required by the lender | Move to holding costs | Not a rehab cost; added to the deal model |
| Buyer's inspection repairs after listing | Set aside a small reserve | Comes out of contingency or profit |

**Outcome:** two items (RRP-certified work and the sewer inspection) were not in the original scope. The investor added them as scope rather than hoping the contingency would absorb them. If the sewer camera finds a failed line, the investor renegotiates or walks before the contingency period ends, instead of discovering it during demo.

## What the result means

- **Every line marked "needs a number" is a risk you have not priced.** Do not make a final offer until each has one.
- **Large findings before purchase are leverage.** A documented sewer or roof problem is a reason to renegotiate.
- **Large findings after purchase come out of contingency first, then profit.** That is the order to avoid.
- **If your contingency is already committed to known items,** your real contingency is smaller than the number on the page.

## Assumptions

- You can access the house for inspections before your contingency deadline.
- Contractors disclose exclusions when asked.
- Local permit rules are available from the building department.

## Limitations

- **Not exhaustive.** Every house and jurisdiction has its own surprises.
- **No prices.** Costs vary too much by market to list reliably. Use local bids.
- **Not legal or compliance advice.** Confirm permit, code and lead-safe requirements with your building department, your contractors and the EPA's guidance.

## Common mistakes

1. **Skipping the sewer camera** on an older house.
2. **Budgeting permits as one small number** without asking about triggered upgrades.
3. **Hiring uncertified contractors** for paint and trim work on a pre-1978 house.
4. **Assuming listed appliances will be usable.**
5. **Forgetting site logistics:** dumpsters, toilet, utilities, lockbox.
6. **No budget for the punch list and cleaning.**
7. **Spending contingency on known scope.**
8. **Putting holding and financing costs into rehab,** then counting them again in the profit model.

## FAQ

### What rehab costs do flippers underestimate most?

Systems behind walls and underground (sewer, wiring, supply pipes, subfloors), permit-triggered upgrades, and small logistics costs that add up across a project.

### Should I get a sewer inspection on a flip?

On older houses, a sewer camera inspection is usually worth it. It is inexpensive compared with the cost of a failed line discovered after closing.

### Do permits only cost the permit fee?

No. A permit can trigger code upgrades, plan review, engineering, re-inspections and schedule delays. Ask the building department what your scope will require.

### What is the EPA RRP Rule?

The EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule requires that anyone paid to do work disturbing painted surfaces in homes built before 1978 be certified and use lead-safe work practices. It generally does not apply to homeowners working on their own homes. Check EPA's guidance and hire certified contractors.

### Is contingency enough to cover forgotten items?

Contingency is for unknowns. If an item on this list applies to your house, budget it as scope. Otherwise your contingency is already spent before the job starts.

### Should holding costs be in my rehab budget?

No. Keep holding, financing, closing and tax costs in your deal model. Putting them in rehab makes rehab comparisons misleading and risks double counting.

### How much should I budget for cleaning and staging?

It depends on house size and local prices. Get quotes for a final construction clean, photos and staging, and add them as separate lines.

### What happens if I find a big problem after closing?

It comes out of contingency, then profit. Re-forecast immediately, get bids, and decide whether to change scope before the problem spreads into your schedule.
