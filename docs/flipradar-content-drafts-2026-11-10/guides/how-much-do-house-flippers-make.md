The honest answer is: the data tells you what flippers **gross**, and almost nothing about what they **net**. The most widely cited national figures come from ATTOM, a property data company that tracks homes bought and resold within 12 months. Its numbers are useful. They are also routinely misquoted as profit.

This guide shows what the latest reports say, exactly what they measure, and how to turn a gross figure into something closer to what a flip actually pays.

## Key takeaways

- **ATTOM's Q2 2026 U.S. Home Flipping Report** (released October 1, 2026) puts the typical gross flipping profit at **$60,526** and the typical gross return at **21.5%**, down from $66,932 and 25.7% in Q1 2026 and $71,000 and 27.6% in Q2 2025.
- **For all of 2025,** ATTOM's year-end report puts the typical gross profit at **$65,981** and the gross return at **25.5%**, which it calls the lowest rate since 2008.
- **"Gross" means resale price minus purchase price.** ATTOM's methodology states it does not include rehab costs and other expenses, which "flipping veterans estimate typically run between 20 percent and 33 percent of the property's after-repair value."
- **Net profit is much smaller than gross, and can be negative.** FlipRadar's own hypothetical sample deal has a $150,000 gross spread and about $37,300 of net profit before tax.
- **Price point matters.** In Q2 2026, ATTOM reports typical gross margins of 28% for homes bought for $100,000–$200,000, and a typical **loss** for homes bought for $50,000 or less.
- **Location matters more than averages.** Among large metros in Q2 2026, ATTOM's typical gross margins ranged from 81.5% in Pittsburgh to a small loss in San Antonio.

## How to apply these numbers

1. **Use national figures as context, not as a forecast for your deal.**
2. **Convert gross to net** with your own costs: rehab, buying and selling costs, holding, financing.
3. **Compare your market** to ATTOM's metro and price-band figures if your area is listed, then underwrite your own deal from comps.
4. **Run your deal** in the [flip profit calculator](/tools/flip-profit-calculator) and set a profit floor in the [Maximum Purchase Price guide](/guides/maximum-purchase-price).

## Inputs explained: what ATTOM measures

| ATTOM term | Definition (from ATTOM's methodology) | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| Flip | An arm's-length sale where the same property had a previous arm's-length sale within the last 12 months | Not limited to renovated houses; any 12-month resale counts |
| Gross flipping profit | Flipped (resale) price minus the purchase price | Not net of rehab, financing, holding or transaction costs |
| Gross flipping ROI / profit margin | Gross flipping profit divided by the original purchase price | Not return on your cash, and not after costs |
| Days to flip | Time from purchase to resale | Not the rehab duration alone |

Two more cautions from the reports themselves:

- ATTOM says it **expanded its property record coverage in 2026**, so count-based metrics such as the number of flips "may reflect both market activity and broader data coverage compared to prior periods."
- The "typical" figures summarize very different flips, from quick resales with little work to full renovations.

## Formula: gross vs net

```
Gross profit (ATTOM)   = Resale price − Purchase price
Gross ROI (ATTOM)      = Gross profit ÷ Purchase price

Net profit (your deal) = Resale price − Purchase price − Rehab − Buying costs
                         − Holding costs − Financing costs − Selling costs
Net after tax          = Net profit − Income and any self-employment taxes
```

## Worked example (hypothetical): gross to net

**FlipRadar's sample deal** (hypothetical; see the [flip profit calculator](/tools/flip-profit-calculator) for the full breakdown):

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Resale price | $295,000 |
| Purchase price | $145,000 |
| **Gross profit (ATTOM-style)** | **$150,000 (103% "gross ROI")** |
| Rehab | −$70,000 |
| Buying costs | −$2,175 |
| Points, fees and interest | −$15,640 |
| Taxes, insurance, utilities | −$4,200 |
| Selling costs | −$20,650 |
| **Net profit before tax** | **about $37,300 (about 14.5% of total cost)** |

The gross figure is four times the net. The "103% gross ROI" is a real number by ATTOM's definition and a meaningless one for deciding whether to do this deal.

**Applying ATTOM's own cost range to its 2025 medians (illustrative arithmetic, not a finding):** ATTOM quotes flipping veterans estimating rehab and other expenses at 20% to 33% of after repair value. Applied to the 2025 median flipped price of $325,000, that range is roughly $65,000 to $107,000, which is as large as or larger than the 2025 typical gross profit of $65,981. This does **not** show that the typical flip lost money: the medians are not from the same houses, many flips in the data involve little rehab, and the cost range is a rule of thumb. It does show that the headline gross figure cannot tell you what a typical flip netted.

## What the numbers mean

- **Gross margins have been falling,** according to ATTOM, for about two years through Q2 2026. Less spread between purchase and resale means less room for costs and mistakes.
- **Cheap houses are not automatically better flips.** ATTOM's Q2 2026 data shows a typical loss on homes bought for $50,000 or less, even on a gross basis.
- **Markets differ enormously.** A national average hides metros with very high gross margins and others near zero.
- **What a flipper makes in a year depends on volume, capital and risk,** not just profit per deal. ATTOM's Q4 2025 data averaged 1.25 flips per investor in that quarter. There is no reliable public figure for the typical flipper's annual net income, and FlipRadar will not invent one.

## Assumptions

- ATTOM's figures are reported as published in its press releases; FlipRadar has not audited the underlying data.
- The worked example is hypothetical and uses the same assumptions as FlipRadar's calculators.

## Limitations

- **Gross, not net.** No figure in these reports is after rehab, financing, holding or selling costs.
- **All 12-month resales count as flips** in ATTOM's methodology, whether or not they were renovated.
- **Press-release level detail.** This guide cites only what ATTOM states in its public releases, not any underlying dataset.
- **Data coverage changed in 2026,** per ATTOM, which affects comparisons of flip counts over time.
- **Point in time.** Check ATTOM's latest release for newer quarters.

## Common mistakes

1. **Quoting gross profit as profit.** "The average flipper makes $60,000" is not what the data says.
2. **Comparing your net to ATTOM's gross** and concluding your deal is weak, or the reverse.
3. **Using national figures to justify a deal** in a market with very different margins.
4. **Assuming a lower purchase price means a better return.**
5. **Ignoring taxes** when estimating what you keep. See the [flip taxes guide](/guides/flip-taxes-short-term-gains-basics-not-tax-advice).
6. **Reading flip counts as pure market trends** after a data coverage change.

## FAQ

### How much profit does the average house flip make?

ATTOM's Q2 2026 report puts the typical **gross** profit at $60,526, a 21.5% gross return on the purchase price. Gross means resale minus purchase, before rehab and other costs. No reliable public figure measures typical net profit.

### How much did house flippers make in 2025?

ATTOM's 2025 year-end report puts the typical gross profit at $65,981 and the typical gross ROI at 25.5%, down from $77,000 and 32.1% in 2024.

### Is house flipping still profitable in 2026?

ATTOM's CEO said in the Q2 2026 release that flippers are "still making money in most markets, but the typical return continues to narrow." Whether a specific flip is profitable depends on its purchase price, costs and market. Run the full numbers.

### What is the difference between gross and net flip profit?

Gross is resale minus purchase. Net subtracts rehab, buying and selling costs, holding costs and financing. On FlipRadar's hypothetical sample deal, gross is $150,000 and net is about $37,300.

### Which price range is most profitable for flips?

In Q2 2026, ATTOM reports the highest typical gross margins for homes bought for $100,000 to $200,000 (28%), and a typical loss for homes bought for $50,000 or less.

### Which markets have the highest flip margins?

Among large metros in Q2 2026, ATTOM lists Pittsburgh, Buffalo, New Orleans, Virginia Beach and Philadelphia as having the highest typical gross margins, and San Antonio, Dallas, Austin, Houston and Salt Lake City the lowest.

### How long does a typical flip take?

ATTOM reports a typical 161 days from purchase to resale in Q2 2026, and an average of 163 days in 2025.

### How much does a full-time flipper make per year?

There is no reliable public data on flippers' annual net income. It depends on how many deals you do, your margins, your costs and taxes. Model your own pipeline instead of relying on averages.

### Where do these numbers come from?

ATTOM's quarterly and year-end U.S. Home Flipping Reports, based on sales deed data. Links and release dates are listed in this page's sources.
