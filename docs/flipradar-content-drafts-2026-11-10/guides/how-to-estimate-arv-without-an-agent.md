You do not need an agent to estimate after repair value. You need sold data, a way to verify it, and the discipline to adjust honestly. An agent with MLS access makes the first part faster. They do not make the judgment better, and a commission-motivated opinion is not an independent one.

This guide covers what an agent would normally do for you: finding comps, checking what actually sold and in what condition, and building adjustments from local evidence. The math lives in the [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator). This page is about getting inputs that deserve the math.

## Key takeaways

- **Public records give you sale prices and dates. Listing history gives you condition.** You need both.
- **Know the market before you know the deal.** Pick a farm area, watch every sale and every flip in it, and your ARVs get faster and better.
- **Verify every comp:** price, date, square footage, condition and concessions. One bad comp can move your ARV by 5% or more.
- **Build adjustments from paired sales** in your own area, not from national tables.
- **Pay for professional help when the stakes or the uncertainty are high.** An appraisal costs far less than an overpaid house.

## How to apply this

### Phase 1: get a pulse on the market

1. **Pick a farm area.** A handful of neighborhoods or zip codes you will watch closely. The smaller the area, the faster you learn what drives value in it.
2. **Set alerts on public portals** for new listings, price changes, pendings and solds in that area.
3. **Track renovated sales in a simple sheet:** address, sale date, price, square feet, beds and baths, garage, finish level, days on market and any concessions you can find.
4. **Watch other investors' flips.** When a house sells cheaply and relists months later renovated, you get a before-and-after sale price, a finish level from photos, and a sense of how long the flip took. Open houses on investor flips show you exactly what buyers in that area are paying for.
5. **Note what sits.** Renovated listings that take far longer than others to sell are telling you about price ceilings and finish expectations.

### Phase 2: estimate ARV for a specific house

1. **Define the finished house.** Beds, baths, square footage, finish level, garage, and anything you will add or remove. Your ARV is for that house, not the one you toured.
2. **Pull sold comps** using the comp rules on the [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator): recent, close, similar size and style, renovated to the same level, arm's-length.
3. **Verify each comp** (see below).
4. **Adjust each comp** to match the finished subject.
5. **Read the spread** as a confidence range and pick a conservative underwriting number.
6. **Sanity-check against active and pending listings** you would compete with.

## Inputs explained: where the data comes from without an agent

| Source | What it gives you | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| County recorder or register of deeds | Deeds, sale dates, recorded prices (in states that disclose them), buyer and seller names | Some states do not disclose sale prices. Recording can lag. No condition data |
| County assessor | Square footage, year built, bedroom and bath counts, lot size | Often outdated. Additions and finished basements may be missing or wrong |
| Public real estate portals | Sold history, listing photos and descriptions, days on market, price changes | Data can be incomplete or wrong. Cross-check price and date against the deed |
| Listing photos and descriptions (current and archived) | Condition and finish level at the time of sale | Photos are marketing. Look for what they avoid showing |
| Title or escrow companies | Some offer property profiles or recent-sale reports on request | Availability varies |
| Appraisers and investor-friendly agents | MLS sold data, local adjustments, a second opinion | You pay for it, in money or in loyalty |
| Your own drive-bys | Street conditions, traffic, neighbors, view, lot usability | Takes time. Worth it on the final three comps |

### How to verify a comp

- **Price and date:** match the portal against the recorded deed when your state discloses prices.
- **Concessions:** look for language in the listing about seller credits. If you can, ask the listing agent. A sale with large seller credits overstates value.
- **Condition:** look at the photos from that sale, not the current photos. A comp that sold dated and was renovated afterward is not a renovated comp.
- **Square footage:** use the same source for the subject and every comp.
- **Arm's-length:** exclude family transfers, foreclosure auctions, bank sales and short sales unless your subject will be sold the same way.

### Building adjustments from paired sales

A paired sale is two sales in the same area, close in time, that differ mainly in one feature. The price difference is evidence of what buyers pay for that feature.

- Two similar renovated ranches on the same street, a few weeks apart, one with a two-car garage: the difference suggests a garage adjustment.
- Two similar houses, one with 1.5 baths and one with 2: a bath adjustment.
- The same house sold twice, before and after a market shift: a time adjustment.

You will rarely find a perfect pair. Collect a few imperfect ones and look for a consistent range. Write the adjustment and the evidence in your comp sheet so you use the same number every time.

## Formula

```
Adjusted comp price = Comp sale price ± Adjustments (comp better: subtract; comp worse: add)
Comp $/sq ft        = Adjusted comp price ÷ Comp square footage
ARV estimate        = Average comp $/sq ft × Subject square footage
Confidence range    = Lowest and highest comp $/sq ft × Subject square footage
Underwriting ARV    = A number at or below the middle of the range
```

## Worked example (hypothetical)

FlipRadar's sample property: a 1,500 sq ft, 3 bed / 2 bath ranch with a one-car garage, to be renovated to a mid-level finish. All numbers are hypothetical.

**What public data showed:** six sales in the subdivision in the last six months. Two were excluded: one was a family transfer at a low price; the other's photos showed original 1970s finishes. One was a sale more than five months old with a lighter renovation, kept as a backup.

**Paired-sale evidence from the farm sheet:**

- Two renovated ranches on the same street sold five weeks apart for $289,000 and $297,000. The higher one had a two-car garage; otherwise similar. Evidence for a garage adjustment of about $8,000.
- Two renovated houses with 1.5 and 2 baths: a difference of about $7,500.

**Final three comps after verification and adjustment:** adjusted prices of $297,000, $296,500 and $298,000, giving an ARV of about $297,400 and a size-normalized range of about $287,000 to $307,000. Underwriting ARV: **$295,000.** The full comp grid is on the [ARV calculator](/tools/arv-calculator) page.

**What changed the answer:** without the photo check, the dated sale would have entered as a comp and pulled the ARV down. Without the concession check, a $305,000 sale with seller credits would have pulled it up. Verification moved the number more than the math did.

## What the result means

- **Tight cluster after adjustments:** you have a usable ARV. Underwrite at or slightly below the middle.
- **Wide spread:** you do not know the value yet. Find the reason, replace the outlier, or underwrite at the low end.
- **Your ARV is above every active listing of similar finish:** your buyers will see cheaper options. Expect a longer sale or a lower price.
- **You cannot find three renovated comps:** the market may not support a renovated price at your finish level. Get a professional opinion before you buy.

### When to pay for help

Pay for an appraisal, a broker price opinion or an hour of an experienced agent's time when:

- The house is unusual: very large or small, rural, multifamily, historic, waterfront, or has an accessory unit.
- Your comps disagree by more than about 10%.
- Your profit depends on a value at the top of the neighborhood.
- It is your first deal in that area.

A professional opinion is cheap insurance against the single most expensive mistake in flipping: paying for an ARV that is not there.

## Assumptions

- Your state discloses sale prices, or you have another reliable source of them.
- Comps reflect arm's-length sales to retail buyers.
- Listing photos fairly show condition at the time of sale.
- Your rehab will match the finish of your comps.

## Limitations

- **Non-disclosure states** do not publish sale prices in public records. You will need MLS data through an agent, an appraiser, or another paid data source.
- **Public portals make mistakes.** Treat them as a lead, not a source of record.
- **Paired sales are rare and imperfect.** Adjustments are estimates.
- **Point in time.** Your ARV is for the market in your comps. If the market moves during your hold, so does your sale price.

## Common mistakes

1. **Using current photos to judge a past sale's condition.**
2. **Skipping the deed check** and trusting a portal's price or date.
3. **Ignoring concessions.**
4. **Using assessor square footage for one house and listing square footage for another.**
5. **Adjusting the subject instead of the comps.**
6. **Borrowing adjustments from another market** or from a generic online table.
7. **Keeping a comp because it helps the deal.**
8. **Not walking or driving the final comps.** A busy road or a power line easement does not show up in data.

## FAQ

### Can I estimate ARV without MLS access?

Yes. County records and public portals give you sold prices and dates in most states, and listing photos give you condition. In non-disclosure states, you will need an agent, an appraiser or a paid data source for sale prices.

### Where can I find sold comps for free?

County recorder and assessor websites, and the sold sections of public real estate portals. Cross-check portal data against the recorded deed where prices are disclosed.

### How do I know a comp was renovated?

Look at the listing photos and description from the sale you are using. Archived photos and the listing history on public portals often show the house as it was when it sold.

### How do I adjust comps without an appraiser?

Use paired sales: two similar sales in the same area that differ mainly in one feature. The price difference suggests the adjustment. Collect several pairs and use a consistent number.

### What are seller concessions and why do they matter?

Seller-paid credits toward the buyer's costs. They raise the recorded price above what the seller really received. Subtract them to get a true comp price.

### What is a farm area?

A small set of neighborhoods you watch closely. Tracking every sale and flip in it builds the local knowledge that makes ARV estimates faster and more accurate.

### Should I trust an online home value estimate?

Only as a rough check. Automated estimates value the house as it is today using broad data, not the renovated house you will sell.

### When should I pay for an appraisal?

When the property is unusual, your comps disagree, the profit depends on a top-of-market value, or it is your first deal in the area.

### How many comps do I need?

At least three good, verified ones. Five is better if they are genuinely similar. More weak comps do not add accuracy.
